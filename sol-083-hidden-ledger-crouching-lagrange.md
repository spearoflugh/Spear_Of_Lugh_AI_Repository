# Hidden Ledger, Crouching Lagrange

City-states were all the rage before nation-states. In what is now Italy, they had the noticeable habit of turning into large empires that shaped the world. Even when the most successful, Rome, fell, a few notable others took up the torch and continued the tradition, like Florence, Venice or Genoa. Those cities basically invented the juridical and financial tools that would make capitalism a thing: bills of exchange, double-entry bookkeeping, marine insurance and the *commenda* contract, public banks and negotiable debt… Things later accelerated with the Great Discoveries, Holland with the stock exchange and the *Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie* (Dutch East India Company) at first, then even more with the Industrial Revolution in the UK.

​We are now living through a new revolution led by new information technologies. The idea that I want to explore in this piece is this: what are the equivalents of the tools that are mentioned for our times? Our times are also filled with great discoveries and new ways to do things: from social media to AI, passing through crypto and distributed systems (not forgetting the space industry), radical changes are happening at the same time. The change is not only in scale but in nature. Just one example is enough to prove the case: the iPhone was released in 2007. In less than a generation, every human is equipped with a similar tool and has 24/7 access to the internet. There is nothing approaching such a brutal change in the whole known history of humanity.

### Knowledge, will, hopes and Destiny

Markets are a distributed way to gather knowledge through the price mechanism. The law of supply and demand is the fundamental one to reckon with in this realm. Say**,** an equivalent of the gravitational and electromagnetic equations, but for the world of ideas. Now**,** in the same way that you can use those equations to build machines to master the material world, you can build a whole industry around markets to do things more efficiently. Basically, what we call finance. The whole game is to work on the price discovery mechanism in order to extract value. It is an ongoing optimization process of refinement of “what is known to achieve aims”. If you want to do X**,** you may consider different alternatives to reach it. Prices help you navigate your way to find the most efficient one, at the time when the action is performed, in this endeavor.

​Note that human nature plays a first-order role here: human greed is the fundamental force behind markets. It is this impulse to want more that sets things in motion. The motion follows the law of supply and demand given the circumstances (regulations, weather events in the case of crop yields, state of human and local knowledge, etc.).

The question is: what are the equivalents for the digital revolution? Before being able to think properly about this question, one must first define what is intended by the term “digital revolution”. I identify two fundamental points:

- ​Loss of speech locality properties. Information used to be hard to retain and to broadcast. Those properties are now marginally free. As soon as you have a smartphone and network access, you can gather, produce, instantly disseminate, and record as much information as you need to at 0 extra cost. The “as much information as you need to” means something like orders of magnitude greater than the whole content of the Library of Alexandria or of the Library of Congress, for that matter.
- Rise of (semi-)autonomous machines. AI and smart machines (say, any dumb version of any machine to which you grant access to the network) in general fundamentally differ from previous machines in that they display various forms of autonomy. In short, they don’t just react to the actions of the humans driving them, but also to inputs coming from the network (e.g., the autonomous Tesla car is getting constant updates from other driving experiences, just like AI responses evolve just by interacting with other users, by the way).

Those two points have a tremendous impact on society as a whole. They change how culture is built (how information is going to be delivered to future generations), how work is organized (the development of distributed systems), the fitness of institutions, etc. Take the example of the idea of justice. A basic principle of our judicial systems is that you apply the law of the land. If you move to Thailand and commit a crime over there, first, it is maybe not a crime in your country (think of all the different legislation about drugs, for instance), second, you are going to be judged following Thai processes. There might exist rules between your country and Thailand regarding extradition, whether or not you can do jail time in your home country, etc., but the idea remains. There is huge importance to where the action took place. Now, what does it mean on the internet? If I am traveling to the US and make a mean tweet mocking Ursula von der Leyen, what rule applies? Am I covered by the 1st Amendment? What if I were in fact in France and were using a VPN? The loss of locality is on full display. And it is just a stupid example.

​But those points also have huge internal consequences for each of us. AI, in the form of LLMs, has been part of the landscape for less than half a decade. Yet every kid has already interacted with them to “work” on homework. Unlike a book that you have to read, you just have to ask the AI to do the work for you. Basically, new information tech users are getting more and more passive.

This last remark can be seen as a trade-off between will and friction. You let the machine make decisions for you, you reply “yes” or “continue” to the LLM suggestions, in order to have your report written without having to sweat about it.

### Under appreciated

Note also that the machine observes and records your interactions with it in order to improve itself. It is as if it sucks the will out of users. Social media, pre-LLMs, were optimized to get your attention. With LLMs and agentic AI, a new step has been crossed: it is the life impulse that is stolen from you.

​This goes much further than a short-term/long-term dilemma. Making the machine think for you changes your way of thinking. The right allegory is the difference between playing and listening to music. As the graphic above shows, you get better grades for homework only to crash later on exam scores. Eliminating friction comes with its own cost.

At the time of writing this trade-off  remains implicit and under discussed. You may feel it but it is never clearly stated. Just like the negative downstream consequences for social media, that I am exploring in this Substack. It is a recurring feature of digital culture. Maybe it started with Google Search who used your history of searches to build profiles in order to optimize. Same story could be told about Facebook and targeted advertising.

Markets resolved the problem of coordinating dispersed knowledge through the price mechanism; the digital revolution has created new information asymmetries (between the user and the platform, between the short and long term) that do not yet have an equivalent discovery mechanism.

It gives us a hint at what an equivalent of market could be: a place to discuss, inform and measure those silent asymmetries. Can  a part be mathematically formalized (and hence tools built to assess them)? I think so but at the time of writing it remains speculative and active research area.

The result will not be a single thing. Like a market is not a thing: there are parts that are psychological (why do this human wants this product so much), others biological (one needs to eat to stay alive), others mathematical (algorithm to compute the price at a given time from demand and supply) other speculative (futures and derivatives) etc. Hence one should expect a set of practices and tools rather than a silver bullet solution.

### Lagrange points

Another aspect of digital life is that it changes on a second-by-second basis. The evolution is faster than in real life, where atoms show inertia. Electrons, on the other hand, can switch direction on a whim. The rapidity with which trends are developing and simultaneously disappearing on social media is telling. Hence, a second major feature appears: where can you find stability? I am using the Lagrange point allegory for those places where nothing moves in a space characterized by eternal turmoil.

​My short answer is: reputation. Reputation here is the total history of an online actor (could be a human or a machine). It is an imperfect tool—past performance is not indicative of future results, as they say—but it is the best one we have yet. What we have today are rather proxies for this proxy: the number of followers, engagement statistics, ratios, etc. As I have explored in this Substack, setting up a reputation system is a tricky endeavor. For one, humans have evolved to game reputation systems. The recent events in academia where plagiarism cases are uncovered day after day are the best practical proof one could ask for. Moreover, it is not even clear that reputation on, say, Facebook (where your network is supposed to be made of people you know) is the same thing as on X (where your network is rather gravitating around ideas and interests).

​In a context where everything moves, reputation is the only fixed point that remains. On social media, trends move at the speed of light, and if one wants to be heard, there is no other choice than to discuss the subject du jour. How one reacts, though, could/should remain an invariant. One needs stable points to orient oneself, and reputation is the only signal that keeps stability across time.

​The list is short: mechanisms to uncover silent asymmetries and reputation systems. Yet, they are the map and compass for the next centuries.
