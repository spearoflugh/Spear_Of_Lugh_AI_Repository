# The Dream of a Dream

I am writing these lines on the 15th of June, 2026. Time is of the essence for this piece because it is about the nth deal between the USA and Iran. The fact that *n* is greater than 30 is impressive for a little more than 100 days of war. Yet, it appears that this one is more serious than the previous ones. Why? Because high-level Iranian officials agree that talks are ongoing, and Israel doesn’t appear very happy about the situation. But this is circumstantial evidence, and in wartime, you (the layman) only know what other players want you to know. However, this is not my angle. I want to focus on the mechanics of this sequence, and specifically on the fact that an official text has yet to be published.

The situation is this: we are in the digital era. Trump can tweet live from the lawn of the White House, and everyone will have access to the message in a few seconds. The Iran war has global repercussions; it is a major geopolitical issue. Yet, nothing concrete is known about this conflict. It's FaaS: Fuzziness as a Service.

### The Fuzziness of digital war

François Mitterrand was the French president from 1981 to 1995. By many measures, he can be seen as the country's gravedigger, since no budget has been positive since his time in office. But this is not the subject of this piece. I mention him because he had a favorite saying borrowed from another figure of French history, the Cardinal de Retz

> On ne sort de l’ambigüité qu’à son détriment
>
> (something like “Exiting ambiguity only comes at one's own expense”)

The idea is clear: by keeping things ambiguous, you leave room for maneuver. I am not sure that Trump knows this quote, but he lives by it and calls it *The Art of the Deal*. The list of declarations since the beginning of the war speaks for itself. The war has been won, restarted, or the blockade extended to unblock the Strait of Hormuz (which was not part of the initial motivations but became the most important one), etc.

What I would like to stress here is that this way of handling public statements is not limited to Trump. Do you remember that since the early 2000s, Elon Musk has been talking about going to Mars “in the next few years”? You can spot the same way of presenting AI revolutions through inflammatory claims, such as making intelligence a commodity or the disappearance of whole sectors of labor.

This kind of communication is perfectly suited for the digital age because it combines: a shock-value statement that can be expressed in a single sentence: *“the war is over,”* *“we are landing on Mars,”* or *“Claude is going to end coders,”* etc. and a near-future prediction that remains vague: the time horizon is long relative to the news cycle but shorter than it used to be—typically a few weeks or months. One can go as far as years, but only for major events like Mars, the destruction of the Earth due to global warming, or the like. Events are never clearly dated or defined. A typical example du jour is Trump’s “Ships are starting to move, many loaded up with oil, out of the Strait of Hormuz”. Where ? How long ? What does “many” mean? etc.

If it looks like social media thirst-trapping or rage-baiting content, that's because that is exactly what it is. This leads to the first remark: this way of communicating has gone mainstream. Whether you are the POTUS, the richest guy on Earth, or a social media nano-celebrity trying to make a few bucks on OnlyFans, you package your message the same way. This was not the case before the digital age: a news anchor in the 80s or 90s did not sound like your neighbor. Now, everyone is playing the same game. It's a kind of generalized *MrBeastification* of communication.

The repetitive aspect is important in itself. It lies at the source of three major disruptions. The first is a generalized loss of bearings; when everything sounds the same and is seen on the same level, there is no way to prioritize. This leads to the second point: this way of communicating targets our nervous system by overloading it and forcing it to work overtime (if only to prioritize information). It also loads us with emotions—manipulating hope, rage, thirst, and basic instincts—which is utterly exhausting. The third point is that, in this leveled playing field, it prepares the ground for massive manipulation. When a peace deal with Iran is placed on the same level as UFC fights on the White House lawn or the beginning of a civil war in the UK with racial riots, it is harder to put things into perspective, and everything is weighed solely against the last news cycle.

### A Culture of Irresponsibility

The beginning of the social media era also marked the beginning of cancel culture. Having access to all past statements, being able to search them easily, and hauling them in front of the woke court was all the rage up to the mid-2020s. The era in which we find ourselves now is its polar opposite: whatever the claim, one can just turn it into a meme. It is the unbearable lightness of social media posts. Yet, there is nothing beyond these posts—this has become the primary medium through which laymen access information.

Nowadays, Hunter Biden is seriously considering running for president. Besides being the son of a former president, he was mostly known for his lavish lifestyle and the scandals surrounding his laptop, on which he recorded his exploits.

Are we locked in a perpetual loop between Charybdis and Scylla? At the time of writing I have no real clue. What I am sure of, on the other hand, is that every day is now looking more like the other days. Groundhogs are less fearsome mythical animals than others, yet they will eat you alive. One day at a time.
