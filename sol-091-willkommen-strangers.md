# Willkommen, Strangers

When I was young and reading history books, I was always wondering how it felt to be part of historical events. History is supposed to be about facts. It is more complex, though. Just focusing on a particular point and choosing an angle changes everything.

Yesterday, five guys were placed in custody on the charges of planning a terrorist attack near a RAF base used to strike Iran. I learned about this event on social media. I don’t really know what this is going to turn out to be: a giant nothing, or an event that will be identified as the start of World War III.

The history books of our present are not yet written. There are no chapters or parts yet of the future history books of our days. Yet I know that when the Third World War starts, I will learn about it this way.

The last time I heard about such a move was on September 9th, 2001. Two days before 9/11, Ahmad Shah Massoud was killed by a booby-trapped camera. At the time, it appeared like just another piece of news. Yet it was the start of world-changing events but I only noticed after the 9/11.

Maybe the Fairford event is of the same nature. Maybe not. It is even harder to say now that news is first collected and disseminated via social media

I have the feeling that, as is often the case with this digital revolution, things are upside down. Nowadays, every news event feels like a potential catalyst for an earth-shattering sequence. Yet, most often, nothing of substance—or at least not on that level—occurs. It has even become a meme: the “nothing ever happens” meme.

We are in this paradoxical era where you have, simultaneously:

1. Everything is known instantaneously,
2. The world is changing faster than ever—look at the rate of smartphone first, and AI second, adoption: in a matter of years, you move from 0 users to the whole world (and it’s not a figure of speech, it really is the whole world)—yet, somehow,
3. It appears as if nothing ever happens to the average social media user.

### Relativity

Maybe the explanation is simple and is linked to the relativity of motion. This is trickier than it appears. Followers of Aristotle argued that the Earth could not possibly be moving or rotating. They used a ship as a classic analogy:

- *Their Logic*: According to ancient physics, an object only moves horizontally if a force actively pushes it. Once you release a stone from your hand at the top of a tower, if the earth were moving you would see it on the trajectory of the object.
- *Their Conclusion*: While the stone falls downward toward the Earth, the Earth keeps moving. Therefore, the stone should not land  at the base of the tower.

Galileo provided a neat explanation a few centuries later by introducing the concepts of relative motion and inertia. He proposed the idea to use a moving boat: on the boat and from the pier the stone follows two different trajectories.

In our case, it looks something like this: before social media, you were like someone on the pier, observing boats moving. A falling barrel on the boat appeared to move at the same speed as the boat, whereas to someone on the boat, it appeared to fall vertically.

The boat was the mainstream media: that media was out of reach for the average Joe. Today, Joe has a social media account. He can see the news on his feed before news networks relay them on the waves. Joe is in the boat, and as far as he knows, the barrel falls vertically.

Joe used to look at the world and its evolution; nowadays, Joe lives it from the inside. It requires introspection rather than observation to notice the changes in the world.

Introspection is much harder to perform because you are the tool performing the observation, and, like the boat, you are not the same as yesterday. I am always struck by buildings. When I go to give classes at my *alma mater*, I see the same buildings. Yet I also see professors who were there when I was a student. They have changed: mostly lost hair and gained waistline — *tempus edax rerum* —. Inside, I still feel like the same person I was as a student. But I changed; the process was mostly unnoticed by myself.

### Internalisation

It took a whole Bible to internalize the idea of God. From a voice in the sky, a burning bush to Luke 17:-20-21

> *20Once, on being asked by the Pharisees when the kingdom of God would come, Jesus replied, “The coming of the kingdom of God is not something that can be observed, 21 nor will people say, ‘Here it is,’ or ‘There it is,’ because the kingdom of God is in your midst.*

How long it will take for society to become self-aware is the story being written, one scroll at a time. People are holding their breath.

​This isn’t only happening on the geopolitical stage. On an intellectual level, famous mathematicians are wondering about the meaning of their lives now that AI is solving problems in an afternoon that took them a lifetime. In the literary realm, one of the most prestigious prizes, Prix Goncourt, was almost awarded to an author who ChatGPTed his book: the Parisian intelligentsia doesn't know what to think about it. In the political realm, all tough decisions are pushed aside, and discussions focus on unimportant details. Just a few years ago, simply posting “learn to code” was enough to get you banned. Nowadays, even “learn to prompt” sounds outdated.

Everywhere you look, it’s the same collection of lost people looking for landmarks that are disappearing one after another.

### Cabaret and Nero

But such atmospheres are not new in human history. As a matter of facts they are to be encountered very often. From Nero —who publicly married a freedman named Sporus, whom he had castrated, dressed in imperial women's attire, and treated as his wife— to late Weimar’s  Berlin which was famous for progressive art (Bauhaus, Expressionism), nightlife, gay and trans subcultures, and female liberation (the “New Woman”) passing by Jericho’s downfall that was framed as a divine judgment against the city's sins and the moral degradation of Canaanite culture.

Bienvenue au Cabaret.
