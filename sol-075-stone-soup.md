# Stone Soup

There exist many versions of the Stone Soup tale. It can be seen from a moral point of view—the enhancement of cooperation through a single ‘magic trick’—or, more cynically, as con men tricking a whole village into feeding them. Interestingly, both interpretations help to understand the current social media atmosphere. Let’s first give a bird’s-eye view of the tale. It goes like this:

**The Arrival**

- The Setting: Weary travelers (often soldiers returning from war) arrive in a village.
- The Conflict: The villagers are suspicious and stingy; they hide their food, claiming they have nothing to spare for strangers.

**The Hook**

- The Proposal: The travelers announce they are going to make a delicious "Stone Soup" for everyone, and all they need is a large pot of water and a stone.
- The Curiosity: The villagers, skeptical but intrigued by the idea of magical soup, agree to provide the pot and the water.

**The Collaboration**

- The "Enhancement": As the water boils, the travelers taste the soup and remark, "It's wonderful, but it would be even better with a few carrots."
- The Contribution: A villager, wanting to see the "magic" work, brings out some carrots from their hidden stash.
- The Escalation: The process repeats throughout the story. The travelers suggest other ingredients—onions, potatoes, meat, herbs—and one by one, the villagers contribute their own hidden food items to the pot.

I am now going to examine how this structure can be found in many instances in our today's digital culture. The first example that comes to mind is the war against the USA/Israel and Iran.

### 14 reasons why

A Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) has just been signed in Versailles by Trump in order to start a process that could end the war with Iran. The story of how we got there is strikingly similar to that of Stone Soup.

​Operation Midnight Hammer (MH) was launched 12 months ago and was followed by the 12-day war. Things weren’t clear at the time. Why then? Why stop now? To what end? Those questions were neither really asked nor properly answered. This is the “magic” part of the magic stone. In this version of Stone Soup, you had the “head of the snake” and “the world will be safer” narratives going on for months following MH. The magic stone was going to bring peace, if not prosperity, to all forever. This is where the digital aspect takes its toll. Since the beginning of a war that has already been “won” 39 times, social media has started to produce narratives to fill the gaps in the official account of the war. And you can see the results: every faction has projected its own explanations onto what was going on. The fact that the war was started by two allies who didn’t share the same views on the very aim of the conflict is now explicit: Israel is clearly not happy, and Trump was seeking fast and visible results (à la Venezuelan).

​The content of the MOU consists of 14 items. This is a laundry list ranging from Lebanon to nuclear enrichment, passing by the lifting of sanctions and what is going to happen—or not—in the Strait of Hormuz. Just like in Stone Soup, you find a lot of diverse ingredients, coming from different horizons, in the pot. It appears that this is not accidental in this geopolitical version of the story. Different actors come for different reasons, but they all want to appear to be the ones leading. Just like the stone, they focus attention and arrange stories as things progress. This is made easier by a digital culture in which news cycles make people lose track and rely on feelings rather than an assessment of what has actually been achieved versus what has been told.

### Diet

This scheme can be observed *ad nauseam* in our new digital regime. Understanding why this is the case is complex; many things, perhaps most, are beyond our comprehension. One thing is certain: the digital revolution makes this behavior not only possible but also rewarding for its proponents—at least in the short term. On a longer timeframe, it rather looks like finger food: three seconds in the hand, three hours in the stomach, and three extra pounds on the scale.

​After the magical dinner, the villagers will come to their senses and notice that they have been conned into cooperating. Perhaps it heralds better days for the village and a new era of cooperation. On the geopolitical side of this story it means that institutions and their leaders are increasingly perceived as useless. Political life is more complicated than a soup, though, and institutional instability will not be fixed by the next meal. This reminds me of the scene in Dostoevsky’s *The Idiot* in which an execution is depicted. The scene is told through the eyes of the condemned man. Time slows down. There is that last meter before the scaffold, the last step, the last drop of sweat. Then, Myshkin wonders what life would be like if the convict were released. Would life seem eternal to him? When the village realizes the trick, what is going to happen?
