# What We Carry Out of the Ruins

Money can be viewed as a generalized claim on what others can do for you in the future. It only makes sense within a society in which trust is not completely destroyed. The bad news is: trust is eroding everywhere your look can rest on. We are at the dawn of a new system and the only thing that we know for sure now is that it will emerge after the collapse of the ancient system. Here is what the richest man on earth has to say about it:

[Embedded X post]

I am afraid that things are not that simple Mr. Musk. I even suspect that you know more than what you are telling about it too.

The central spine of Western history lies in the continuous transformation of trust across the ages. It began, for what we know, in the Bronze Age, when long-distance trade routes first emerged. Money was as objective as it could get: transactions were settled by fixed weights of metal. Classical Greece transformed this barter-heavy exchange by inventing minted coinage, maritime insurance, and commercial law, which Imperial Rome scaled into a massive, unified global market made possible by standardized legal contracts and corporate-like entities. Following the fall of antiquity, the Champagne fairs and medieval Venice pioneered paper-based finance (bills of exchange) and modern financial engineering—developing double-entry bookkeeping, foreign exchange contracts, and banking houses—while Northern Europe’s Hanseatic League established merchant cartels and standardized cross-border maritime law (*Lex Mercatoria*). The Dutch Republic ushered in early modern financial capitalism by launching the world’s first publicly traded joint-stock corporation (the VOC), central banking, and stock exchanges. Abstractions continued to evolve along the same lines under the British Empire, and finally the USA, which integrated these institutions into today’s global system.

We are reaching the end of the road for the US-centric system. Lights are flashing red across the control board: a sovereign debt crisis, demographic collapse, and the end of the petrodollar system—the last evolution of the monetary regime dating back to the 1970s, when, amongst other factors, the French reclaimed their gold, the only financial asset that is its own collateral, and nudged Nixon to decouple the dollar from gold—accelerated by the conflict with Iran. An academic restructuring is on the horizon (OpenAI solved hundreds of open math problems), along with growing distrust in political institutions across the empire (people no longer trust elections; the latest example being when Bolsonaro fled after losing to Lula in Brazil—and perhaps Lula would have had to flee had Bolsonaro won, after voting was mysteriously extended by two hours), as widespread unrest in the periphery of the empire illustrates. Trust is collapsing everywhere at once, from local municipal boards to the geopolitical stage, extending even to school boards.

The natural question is to contemplate what monetary, financial, political, and global system could come next. The exploration of this question is a huge subject on which I plan to work across many pieces. In this piece I am setting the stage of this discussion.

First let us establish a schematic view of “the system.” I base my perspective on how things work on a dualist philosophy that I explained in

[Linked post: Towards a Digital Society Manifesto - Part 1]

In this text, I have presented a vision of society divided into four fundamental tiers based on how humans interact across the virtual and physical worlds. It rests on a dualist metaphysics that interprets the world through two primary categories—ideas and objects—and their interactions. In a nutshell:

- **Warriors**, who directly act on the physical world to turn ideas and will into concrete reality (such as the executive branch).
- **Priests**, who interpret the objective world, construct meaning, and frame physical events within moral structures (such as the judiciary branch). From another angle, price signals are the transformation of objective reality into ideas; hence, financiers belong to the priest category.
- **Merchants**, who operate in the physical world to sustain everyday life and drive the material economy (ranging from manual laborers to small business owners, engineers, and industrial founders).
- **Clerks**, who work entirely in the realm of ideas as intellectuals, columnists, and traditional gatekeepers of information whose editorial power has waned somewhat in the digital age. They are the ones who build the moral frameworks used by priests to interpret reality.

One can also frame this vision through an industrial lens. When an order becomes industrialized, it transforms into a complex: the Military-Industrial Complex (MIC) represents the warrior class, the Financial-Industrial Complex (FIC) the priest class, the Techno-Industrial Complex (TIC) the merchant class, and the Social Media Complex (SMC) the clerk class. Naturally, these are not rigid distinctions, yet they remain reasonably clear. Take a company like Raytheon: it manufactures highly advanced technological products that are directly tied to warfare, and as a major corporation, its market valuation inherently introduces a financial dimension. Despite these overlaps, Raytheon remains fundamentally rooted in the MIC.

These complexes should be viewed as primitive forces shaping our social environment. Things would be easier if they were not *complex*. Here, the word must be understood as both a noun and an adjective: they are systems made of many moving parts, intricately developed. To make matters even messier, there are numerous feedback loops between them. The most obvious is the relationship between finance and power: money can buy power (through lobbying or bribing legislators), and power can yield money (just look at stock indices tracking Nancy Pelosi’s investments). Likewise, the FIC and TIC constantly interact; entire new sectors of the economy (such as the internet economy or space exploration ventures like SpaceX) were only able to launch thanks to loans, tax exemptions, and subsidies.

### Situation Report

Before tackling the main issue, let’s further examine where we stand in the grand course of history through the lenses of the MIC, FIC, TIC, and SMC, and how the new information technology revolution impacts each of them.

By its very nature, the TIC is ahead of the other complexes in this transformation. This is evident on a daily basis in the prominence of CEOs like Musk, Altman, Amodei, and Thiel, not only within the global discussion but also through their direct geopolitical influence (e.g., the importance of Starlink in the ongoing Ukraine-Russia war or the use of Claude by the Houthis to ramp up their military capabilities).

The MIC has caught on to the digital revolution as well: Anduril and Palantir Technologies are two famous examples. Likewise, the SMC has completely transformed political communication, at least since the first Trump administration. The FIC, despite advances in DeFi, appears to lag behind the other complexes, though it too has been impacted by the digital revolution. We will see later why it appears to be the case.

All these transformations are remarkably abrupt, not only in their speed and scale but also in their reach. Century-old institutions are falling before our eyes, and stability is nowhere in sight. Consider just one issue: how will academia evolve? Academia is crucial in this framework because it was the most visible arm of the SMC prior to the digital revolution, while also serving as the foundation for the other complexes. Fundamental discoveries made in university laboratories directly enabled technological applications (think of the transistor or DNA mapping); the discovery of the neutron led within a bit more than a decade to thermonuclear weapons, profoundly impacting the MIC; and a significant share of Decentralized Finance research continues to originate in academia (through mathematics, cryptography, etc.).

The academic structure, both its research and its teaching arms, is no longer viable, for two reasons. The traditional model of academic research—funded by grants that rely on peer-reviewed articles, commonly known as the “publish or perish” culture—is no longer viable for two key reasons. First, AI has made writing research papers so effortless that it is clogging the system; abundance brings its own set of problems. Second, the fundamental nature of learning in a digital society is shifting in real time: are traditional methods still relevant? Packing students into large lecture halls is hardly optimal anymore. Likewise, what should be taught is equally uncertain. Consider this: “learn to code” was viewed as the future of work just a decade ago; today, it is widely seen as obsolete, or at the very least marginal in terms of industrial needs, given AI’s rapidly advancing coding capabilities.

### Trust in digital societies

Now that the foundations have been laid, we can tackle the central question: how is trust going to evolve in a digital culture?

There is a profound paradox at play: establishing trust is, by definition, a slow process, and inherently asymmetrical. Building trust and destroying it are two completely different things: the former is incremental, while the latter is driven by discrete events. This stands in stark contrast to the rapid pace of digital advancement. Moreover, many innovations are tested and either adopted or discarded in real time. This dynamic may explain why the adoption rate of Bitcoin and various DeFi innovations does not compare to that of platforms like Airbnb or Uber. It is one thing to trust a platform for a one-off experience; it is quite another to switch to a new meta-infrastructure like a monetary system, which is in constant use.

Another hard fault line is the globalist/nationalist divide between digital and written based culture. Trust in authorities appear natural but are historically relatively new. The evolution of literacy, for once,  changed the structure of political power: the whole history of the 19th and 20th centuries can serve as a warning. Such large scale changes do come with their share of traumatic events, think world wars, bolshevik revolution and that kind of things. By nature digital culture does not rely on geographic constraints while previous power structures did. How this major tension is going to be resolved remains an open question. But it will play the major part in the evolution of trust at a societal level.

A subtle point linking the two previous points is the following: right now legacy institutions are concentrating the societal trust mechanisms. It seems sensible than those institutions will spend this accumulated trust capital by adapting. One example could be the development of state based crypto currencies. One can note that the fast adoption of Airbnb and Uber was facilitated by the fact that those platforms relies on the existing trust architecture: legal framework, banking system, police etc.

Mythology gives us an idea of what happens in such dire times.

#### Protocolizing Trust Among Strangers - A Case Study

When transitioning from one era to another, the  mechanisms for establishing trust must be completely re-engineered.

The Homeric Iliad/Odyssey can be seen as the story of the bridge between two radically different trust architectures:

- ​Bronze Age Trust (Top-Down & Material): Mycenaean Greece ran on a centralized palace-economy controlled by a *king*. Trust was architectural and administrative: linear clay tablets (Linear B), strictly controlled storehouses, physical gold redistribution, and bureaucratic oversight.
- ​Dark Age Collapse: When the palaces collapsed around 1200 BCE, Linear B disappeared. Institutional trust disappeared with it.
- ​The Homeric Transition**:** The epics introduce a peer-to-peer reputation architecture. In the absence of state infrastructure, trust is built through *Kleos* (imperishable glory/reputation) and *Timê* (honor/status). The *Iliad* is an exhaustive study of what happens when this decentralized trust network breaks down—specifically, when Agamemnon violates *Timê* by taking Achilles’ reward, causing the entire alliance protocol to crash.

During the Bronze Age, security came from walled citadel-states. If you left your city, you were on your own or an outlaw. In the fragmented, post-collapse Mediterranean, long-distance trade and diplomacy required a protocol for interacting with non-kin without immediately resorting to violence.

The protocol codified in the epics is to see Xenia (guest-friendship) as a sacred ritual protocol:

- Welcome the stranger and feed them before asking their name or business.
- Exchange gifts (Keimelia) of verifiable, durable value (e.g., bronze cauldrons, silver cups).
- Form a hereditary pact of mutual protection.

In the Odyssey, Xenia operates like a web of trust. A guest carries no ID, but by invoking a shared lineage or presenting a inherited gift from a past host (as Glaucus and Diomedes do in the Iliad), trust is instantly verified across generations and distances.

The Iliad is about the breakdown of political consensus. The Odyssey is about identity verification. Odysseus returns home to an environment overflowing with zero-trust dynamics: his palace is occupied by hostile suitors, and his wife and subjects assume he is dead.

Odysseus cannot simply state who he is; he must prove it through layered challenge-response authentication and cryptographic-like identity verification:

- Biometric Proof: The old nurse Eurycleia recognizes him by the scar on his thigh (a permanent, non-forgeable physical mark).
- Skill / Cryptographic Proof: Stringing and shooting his custom bow—an asset calibrated only to his physical capability.
- Shared Secret / Knowledge Proof: Penelope authenticates him using the secret of their bed, built directly into the roots of a living olive tree—an piece of private data known strictly to the two of them.

But the most significant trust transformation is technological: moving from oral culture to written culture. Let’s examine the differences:

- Oral Trust Architecture: Before widespread literacy, trust was preserved via human memory—bards who memorized thousands of lines using formulaic meter. Trust resided in performance, shared myth, and living memory.
- Written based Trust Architecture: The Iliad and Odyssey were among the first major works written down using the newly adapted Greek alphabet. By standardizing these tales into written text, Greek culture moved from a fluid, dynamic memory network into a static, verifiable single source of truth. This common text served as the cultural infrastructure for Pan-Hellenic identity, allowing disparate city-states (Athens, Sparta, Corinth) to trust a shared civilizational baseline.

### Archetypes

History helps us to capture archetypes. Somehow we find ourselves at the fall of Troy. Hundreds, if not thousands of books will be written on the whys and hows. Even if, as individuals, we have little impact on those tectonic shifts, our agency remains. Heroes exist to remind us that individual decisions matter. Our primary duty is not to abdicate in the face of inertial forces.

​Xenia could not scale up to cities and large populations. It evolved through the development of cities and laws, but also through commercial hospitality and monetary economics: you could now pay for your room and dinner at a tavern. Travelers no longer had to rely on hospitality as a sacred moral obligation. On a cultural level, the ancient religious enforcement of xenia evolved into the broader concept of philoxenia (literally, “love of strangers”). This became the cultural foundation for modern hospitality, tourism, and international diplomatic protocols.

The global migrant crisis, among other crises, shows us that we have reached the end of a trust system. Worldwide travel is easy and cheap. The idea of the modern citizen was developed during the French Revolution: it was done at a time when migrants represented a tiny minority. This is no longer true. The modern citizen was an authentication system built for a world of single, exclusive belonging. Today, belonging is plural and identities are hard to verify, exactly the problem Xenia once solved among strangers.

​The new architecture of trust will necessarily depend on digital technologies. But beware: those same digital technologies can be used to game trust. This breakdown manifests itself when the harmony among the four classes is broken. The powers that be can use intrusive identification mechanism in order to better control subjects. One way or another the modern citizen status is going to vanish.

​The digital hero’s journey is to help re-establish this harmony. It won’t be easy, and it won’t be done by throwing money at outdated methods. Most Western states are crumbling under debt as a result of this misalignment. An unfair system is like a lie: as time goes by, it becomes more and more costly.

​The walls of Troy, no matter how large, cannot withstand the assault of destiny. Troy will fall; that is not ours to decide. What is ours is what we carry out of the ruins, and what mental models we refuse to be buried with.
