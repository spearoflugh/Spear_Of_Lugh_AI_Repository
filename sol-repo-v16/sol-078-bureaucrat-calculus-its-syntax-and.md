# Bureaucrat Calculus: Its Syntax and Semantics

The shift to a digital culture is happening simultaneously as we reach the endgame stage of bureaucratic evolution. There are no coincidences here. Information technologies have introduced the technological possibility for the craziest extension of the bureaucratic state. The French word for computer is “ordinateur,” which literally means “the one that sorts out/puts things into order.” I am sure that the wet dreams of bureaucrats are full of Excel spreadsheets and boxes to tick. The smartphone evolution has only increased the volume and liveliness of data that can later be used as a control gauge by bureaucrats.

### Keys and locks

From the outside, bureaucratic late-stage life looks like an accumulation of locks. Each data point becomes a new handle of control — a lock for which only the administration holds the key. From this point of view, they are the dual of the Keymaker character from *Matrix Reloaded*. In the movie, the Keymaker acts as a master of “backdoor” access. His primary function is to create and maintain special keys that allow programs to bypass standard security and traverse the Matrix through hidden conduits. By essence, he is the anti-bureaucrat; indeed, their aim is to ensure that everything is under control and that no unauthorized event can ever happen.

An exemple du jour is provided by the French 14th of July military parade: this year, if you wanted to watch it live from the street, you had to have a QR code and be approved by the administration. Talk about Authorization Based Citizenship (for non french readers the Bastille day is supposed to be the symbol of the french Revolution: citizen taking over royalty…). There are more twists to the story, as a judge ruled yesterday that it was illegal. Yet, the bureaucrats were not going to let their power go without a fight and obtained the annulment of the court ruling this morning by appealing to the Conseil d’État. These jolts occur purely within administrative justice, yet they show that an institutional tipping point has been reached. The new powers that be tend to augment their authority using new technologies, without taking into account any bounds other than their ability to implement the rule. Notice that, by doing so, they are at the mercy of private actors (the Apple and Google app stores) and at the end of a technological chain that they do not master.

This example shows the crux of the problem: on one hand, new technologies allow bureaucratic organizations to implement rules in a way that has never been seen in human history; on the other hand, the means by which these rules are implemented are becoming increasingly alien to those administrations. The situation is reminiscent of those start-up companies that are merely a shell around a LLM: they are at the mercy of model or billing updates from OpenAI or Anthropic. From the outside, they appear like giants, but with feet of clay.

Another point worth noting is the hesitation within the administrative apparatus. The fact that one judge ruled it illegal while another found it acceptable shows that we have reached a singularity point. Just as the differential levels of gravity exerted by the moon on the Earth produce tides, the technological revolution is not unfolding at the same speed in every branch of the administration. Some are more conservative than others, which serves as an additional factor of instability.

### The Automation of Control

The bureaucratic is adapting to, even if unevenly, to the digital revolution. It may think it is going to use new technologies for its own good but as it has always been the case, technology will transform bureaucracy as much as writing transformed the structure of powers emanating from an oral culture. The question is to know how the bureaucratic class will evolve? Some trends are already visible:

- **Real time**: it only takes a few hours to set up an app and distribute it among citizens. A QR code is simply a random number recorded in a database that can be queried live. Rules can be created on the fly. This contrasts sharply with what would have happened in a culture based on written documents, where one would have had to actually print and physically distribute them. Of course, bureaucrats will use this to their advantage. Judicial time is too slow to react; imagine you request a QR code for tomorrow—who is going to file a recourse in time? And even if a judge rules it illegal, well, too bad: the event occurred last week. The logical evolution, therefore, is that the administration will wait as long as possible to act, ensuring they are never countered by the judiciary. They will call it an “emergency”.
- **Accountability**: if one had wanted to implement controlled access to the 1938 14th of July parade, one would have had to rely on many people—for instance, police officers who would have had to deny entry to citizens. In 2026, you can hire private security personnel who have no culture other than: “if the light is red, access is forbidden.” Police officers would have been expected to know the rights of citizens and would have been hard-pressed to justify such a ban. In a digital culture, everyone knows that a red light means “no.” You do not know why the light is red, but it *is* red—and machines make no mistakes, right? In short, by using machines, you bypass the distributed layer of administrative accountability. It is no longer a bureaucrat who has to justify the “no”; the machine does it, and the bureaucrat perceives themselves as a mere servant of the system. In this new order, no one is accountable for anything. It goes a step further than the Arendt’s banality of evil or the Weberian rational-legal authority. The system is now a black box , literally this is what the case of your smartphone is, and no one is asking why or whether access control decisions make sense.
- **Predictability**: the last two trends mechanically produce the third—the absence of predictability. It will become increasingly difficult to anticipate bureaucratic decisions. While the global direction is clear and does not favor greater liberty for the average citizen, what was once the center of gravity of society is now becoming a kernel of instability. In concrete terms, this instability will be distributed among private citizens: they will be barred from doing “X” simply because the system says so. Consequently, it will not be a series of huge events, but rather a collection of small-scale dramas occurring on a massive scale. This means that society will become less harmonious, characterized by occasional bursts of discontinuity. Here the astute reader will have recognized a version 3.0 of Deleuze society of control.

Drones and automated systems have changed the battlefield; news from the wars in Ukraine and the Middle East makes that clear. An equivalent transformation is now hitting the internal structure of our nation-states. The nervous system of the state—the administration—is being patched and equipped with microchips. For the moment, one may only see the new capabilities brought by these technologies, but there is a high price to pay. It is like a loan: it gives you more latitude to act today, but you are in debt, and the interest is accumulating. I am pretty sure the interest rate is not fixed either.
