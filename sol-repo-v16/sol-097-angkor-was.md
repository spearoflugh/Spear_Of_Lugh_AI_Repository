# Angkor Was

Many tech enthusiasts are focused on the Kardashev scale. From the planetary to the omniversal tier, they look at energy consumption to classify civilizations. But energy is only a proxy. The most salient feature of our civilization is actually its speed of transformation. Granted, acceleration requires energy, but it must be directed energy. And boy, in terms of acceleration, we have seen plenty of it over recent months. To put things into perspective, let’s see where we were six months ago:

[Linked post: A Maze Inc.]

This time, it is Anthropic that is at it. On top of their high-profile activism along the lines of ‘we are dealing with a technology that could kill us all’, they have apparently drafted an AI safety bill for lawmakers. Note how we moved from ‘we propose our manifesto’ to ‘here is the bill to regulate our industry’ as naturally as a caterpillar turns into a butterfly.

This comes after a terrible few weeks for mathematicians, who are still soul-searching after the October 6th event—the massive OpenAI drop of papers on GitHub solving hundreds of open problems. The fact that the AI solved so many problems in such a short time might appear to be a matter of ego. It actually goes deeper: the very fabric of academia is affected. Indeed, if anyone equipped with AI can produce what was once seen as top-of-the-food-chain results, then why would taxpayer money fund academics? Even more troubling: how do you tell which mathematician is the best? Because the old method—looking at the record of published results—might say more about the AI they used than their own capabilities. As a mathematician, I am not saying that math has been solved by AI. It is more complex than that, and I will write about this issue later. But I am saying that academia as we know it—say, the universities that have been the center of knowledge for our civilization for nearly a millennium—is over. Even if this was to be expected (the whole of this Substack is about this), it happened fast and deserves a moment of reflection.

Fastening your seatbelt is the first move whether in a car, a plane or a rocket that are about to start. Being able to make one with the accelerating artefact is paramount otherwise the force applied on loose parts of the vehicle might lead to its disintegration. Acceleration is force divided by mass says Newton. You have to apply force in order to change the course of events. Inertia is the first law of mechanics right? Well in our digital times, things turn out to be a bit different. Acceleration comes first, force only follows. There is a delay that classical mechanic can't explain. At the image of written culture based institutions trying to fathom the disorder of early digital times.

Institutions are like buildings: heavy structures that are hard to move. Unlike humans, they are meant to withstand the test of time unscathed—except when times change. Think of the Late Bronze Age collapse, the fall of the Western Roman Empire, or the Classic Maya collapse. That kind of stuff. Maybe you haven’t noticed yet, but you are living through one of those eras. When such brutal shifts occur, buildings and institutions are both left to decay. Just take a look at Angkor Wat:

Things don’t just fall apart overnight like in a Hollywood movie. Nature creeps in slowly, even as humans have disappeared. It is this exact disconnect that is hard to grasp when you are at the center of the hurricane. Some parts move slowly while others explode. There is even the eye of the storm, where everything remains quiet.

Here is a picture of another city. Do you know which city it is, without cheating?

It is Nagasaki. This is how the city looked like 80 years ago:

A nuclear blast lasts far less time than a societal collapse. Buildings can be rebuilt faster than culture. What we are going through right now doesn’t impact buildings or institutions in a tangible way. Yet it is all the more troubling because the destabilization process runs deeper in the revolution towards digital culture.

Having Anthropic hold the hand of the legislator is just the amusing part. It is ironic because Anthropic is filled with naive effective altruists and exhibits a worldview reminiscent of a teenager. A more charitable way to put it would be to say that they haven’t taken the time to sit down and think. In their rush to the frontier, they are acting like a bolting horse: after a few moments, the horse doesn’t even remember why it is running, but it had a good reason at first, so it just runs faster.

A more telling allegory is the scene in which the house, and Joel's mental integrity, falls apart in *Eternal sunshine of the spotless mind.*

[Embedded video]

The erasure of written culture is accelerating. The house is falling apart. At the beginning of the movie, Joel seeks to escape the pain by erasing Clementine. In this final scene at the house, he accepts the collapse, but realizes—thanks to Clementine—that he must leave a trace. The tragedy of forgetting transforms into the promise of a new beginning (’Meet me in Montauk’). I am not sure we are quite at this point yet, but it is approaching fast.

​Today, October 17th, is a day of demonstrations in France. You might think it is business as usual for a nation internationally known for its propensity to riot over everything and nothing. Last night, the University of Rouen was set ablaze. Pictures speak louder than words:

The whole world is falling apart—both the intellectual realm and the material infrastructure. Yet the riots seem mostly pointless; people don’t know yet what they want. They are just feeling the unbearable heaviness of being. They won't be able to escape this state of stupor before what happens is captured in a story. Writing this story is our historical duty to humanity's endeavor.
