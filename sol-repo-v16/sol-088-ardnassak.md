# Ardnassak

The new information technology revolution just offered us another episode of a total inversion: this time it is the myth of Cassandra. In the original version she is cursed by Apollo, who gave her the power of divination under the promise of sex. As Cassandra eventually refused. Apollo, unhappy to have been played, spit in her mouth so that no one was ever going to believe a single word she said.

AI doomers are the polar opposite of Cassandra: they are always wrong, yet they are believed by everyone. I will refrain myself to extend the allegory to their intimate live but archetypes are stronger than us. This new anti-archetype was particularly visible during last week’s joint work of OpenAI, Anthropic and Musk to make us believe that the end of humanity was just around the corner. Journalist relayed the talking points without raising any pushback. Just like during the COVID-19 crisis mass media acted as a thoughtless relay of fears. This is, alas, a general phenomenon. Let’s decipher it.

[Image caption: The stage is set]

The stage is set and looks like this: on stage right are the doomers, drunk on their own unfettered imaginations: “ASI is going to kill us all by designing a dreadful virus; it will encode itself in the human cortex by manipulating pixels on screens to bypass air gaps,” and so on. On stage left are the elites (the political class, journalists), listening to this nonsense as if it were gospel.

​Now that the stage is set, the play can begin. The story is familiar: for your safety, emergency measures are needed, and this or that oligarch will be granted special privileges to address the issue.

​The energy deployed by the doomers is rivaled only by the apathy of the elites. The less the elites understand what the doomers are saying, the more they act as though they get it. They put on a very concerned face, lower the pitch of their voices, and somehow find a way to frame the situation as being similar to the late 1930s—the darkest hours of humanity are just around the corner, and you are Chamberlain if you don’t act now. The public tries to follow what is going on, but the script is full of inside jokes and remains unclear. So the public does what it always does: they look at familiar actors and identify with them. On social media, flags, other rallying signs, and talking points blossom.

​Of course, you can replace the AI apocalypse with your own pet scenario: financial crash, climate change, pandemics, crumbling birth rates, peak oil, etc. For each scenario, a group of irreducible fans remains locked in the fiction. Imagine the yearly reunion of Harry Potter enthusiasts: costumes, magic wands, and brooms. Those crowds have always existed, but now, with the internet, their gatherings are turning into a lifestyle. On social media, there is always an awakened guy ready to talk about it and continue the waking dream.

[Image caption: Cosplay as a lifestyle]

This is the digital part of the Ardnassak curse. In the original story, Cassandra knows that the Trojan horse is a trap, of course. In our reality, doomers are the Trojan horse themselves. They are used to harness power without having to say so. After all, it’s for your safety, and this emergency is really serious…

​What does the myth tell us? After the fall of Troy, Cassandra is taken back to Greece as a prisoner and a prize of war (remember tech moguls at Trump’s inauguration?). She foresees the deadly trap awaiting them at Mycenae and predicts both Agamemnon’s assassination and her own—but no one listens because of Apollo’s curse. Upon arriving at the palace, she meets her end almost immediately when Agamemnon’s wife, Clytemnestra, and her lover, Aegisthus, murder both Agamemnon and Cassandra.

​What does this mean for the Ardnassak version? The inversion leaves no room for doubt. The powers that be—the alliance of the old elite with tech oligarchs—will continue to use the Ardnassak way of controlling public discussion.

​In the myth, after the assassination of Cassandra, Clytemnestra and Aegisthus seize total control of Mycenae, ruling together as tyrants. They treat Agamemnon’s remaining daughters, Electra and Chrysothemis, with cruelty and contempt. Electra, to protect her young brother Orestes from Aegisthus, smuggles him out of the kingdom to be raised safely in Phocis. Later, Orestes returns to avenge the death of Agamemnon…

​If our reality continues on its dual path with myth, there is not much room for hope. But Greek myths offer many stories where heroes rebel against fate—Oedipus, Achilles, and Prometheus, for instance. Which one it will be remains unclear. What is clear, though, is that all heroes who try to escape their fate end up fulfilling it through their very attempts.
