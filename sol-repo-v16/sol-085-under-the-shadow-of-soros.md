# Under the Shadow of Soros

Sorites paradox embeds a simple but powerful observation. The basic idea is that you can’t say when a certain amount of rice grains becomes a heap. If you start with a single rice grain, then surely you don’t have a heap. You can add one grain at a time. If you reach 7,364 grains, surely you have a heap. So there is a point where you go from a non-heap to a heap along the way. Which one? The name of the paradox comes from Ancient Greek: *soros* is the word for heap.

​This paradox can be worked in many directions. My take on it for this piece is the following: there is a gap between the world of objects and the one of ideas. We are trying to represent the objective world in our minds, but it doesn’t work as neatly as expected. It is another way to interpret Plato’s allegory of the cave. The shadows at the back of the cave are our mental representations in this perspective.

I am going to tackle statistics and their relation to real life. This piece originates from thoughts about the Arday/Cofnas affair. In the aftermath of the death of Arday, Cofnas was interviewed on several major podcasts, and the discussions rang strangely to my ears. They were about the disparities in average IQ of populations. I am not addressing here the scientific validity of the claim in itself (an affair for specialists). I am rather focusing on the implications of such a statistical indication.

​Of course, the very idea of race is similar to that of a rice heap. For once, race, unlike sex, is a real continuum. I mean it in a mathematical way: by definition, race is inherited, and you can have as many intermediate variants between group A and B. One just has to put a B ancestor deeper in the genealogical tree of an A person to achieve the amount of difference wanted. Hence the question: at what percentage A becomes B is like the question of: when does the heap of rice appear out of grains?

The first point that I address is self-evident but often neglected. You have never talked once in your life to a statistic. You have exchanged with individuals. And the average height of men doesn’t mean anything for you or Victor Wembanyama: individuals are not statistics.

​In the case of weight, the average weight and the weight distribution across a population might help you to design an elevator rated for *x* people. Likewise, average measures can be used to design seats in airplanes, etc. The case of IQ is peculiar, though. Indeed, ideas can be used in a way that material abilities can’t. Consider team A: one genius at 160 IQ teamed with four people at 95 IQ. Now team B is composed of 5 people at 108 IQ. Both A and B have the same average IQ. But I am pretty certain that team A might be more groundbreaking than team B (but maybe team B is more stable or whatever). The genius might be able to use his superior intellect to produce more efficient teamwork with groundbreaking concepts. There is no equivalent in the material world: you can’t spread strength, height, or weight in the way that you can disseminate and use ideas. The genius might help his teammates much better than a strong man can add strength to his team, even if he is the strongest man who has ever lived. This simple remark shows that the average is a very poor indicator in itself. If you don’t have more precise information on the distribution, you don’t know much. It can even be argued that a few outliers are enough to reach similar results.

​The question, in the particular case of intelligence, is even more complex. Consider all the fuss around AGI: are all going to die etc. Notice that it doesn’t rely on the number of AGIs, but on their very existence. It is clear that a single AGI could trigger all those issues that are discussed at length. There is no notion of average IQ for Artificial Intelligence. This adds another layer of complexity that is distinct from the ones emanating by a callous use of statistics.

​This last remark is the perfect segue to a more general understanding of the issues behind the use of statistics. Logic uses connectives like “for all” or “exists”. The mere fact that a measure is statistical brings you beyond those connectives. In the case of average IQ of populations, how do you translate, assuming that it is a real difference, a 10-point difference? In logical terms, it isn’t a “for all” nor an “exists”. Yet we tend to have discussions in which logic is used. Just as is the case with the Sorites paradox, we find ourselves in a dire situation. Physical realities are imperfectly represented in the realm of ideas.

​Those imperfections between ideas and reality are even worse in the case of statistics because the conversations involve different scales. Remarks jump back and forth from individuals to populations. For instance,the remark that I made about the fact that no individual is a statistic is important. During a conversation, I am only interacting with individuals. You can’t exchange with a crowd: you can have a one-way exchange, but it is not a discussion. On top of that, social media, which is where conversations take place nowadays, adds another layer of complexity. Indeed, a message posted by an average or a huge account looks the same. Yet the first one is directed towards a crowd,while the second may retain some aspects of a standard conversation. And things can even change: if I post a message that for some reason becomes viral, this message might change its status live. The nature of an online conversation is more than just a succession of posts, which is another difference with logic or game theory in which the sequence of moves is the only thing to be considered.

​Those subtleties about logic might appear out of touch. After all, people are not using formal methods when they discuss. That might be true, but there is still a feeling of “being right”. And if you are not proven wrong, then it means that you are right. Right? But as we saw, the inherent fuzziness around definitions and statistical information draws a landscape where one can choose to be never wrong. A heap for thee and not for me. Let alone bad faith actors and the dynamics of online discussion.

​A concrete example of what this can entail can be seen in how the COVID crisis unraveled. CFR, infection rates, validity of measures, etc., were all imperfect statistics. Yet the mandates, regulations, and practices (put your mask on when getting up at the restaurant…) were not statistics. They were imposed upon every individual. Ironically,  the new information played a key role in switching from statistics to the individual back and forth. It was used to implement various versions of vaccination passports in one direction, but on the other, it was used to build statistics in real time. Notice also that this information was used to censor online speech. Even the discussion around probable events like the Wuhan lab theory was in effect strictly forbidden.

What can be said about the discussion on IQ average différences between population? Those statistical signals surely exist, in fact  what would be surprising is that they don’t. Yet driving political measures from them appear not very wise. Jason Arday was not an outlier in IQ terms. The fact that he was professor at Cambridge directly derived from flawed policies driven by statistics. His candidature was helped because he was part of a group that was felt as under represented all things considered. As we saw, maybe this apparent underrepresentation is not real (because maybe there are statistical differences in abilities for populations regarding this position that can explain those visible disparities), but even if it were true that such an imbalance existed, having a policy driven by statistical groups is unwise. On the other hand the plagiarism was proved. Those misconducts and lies were not statistical, not of a sorites nature. Yet it connects to the discussion above: given how mediocre the underlying work was, the sheer scale of the fraud leaves little room for an explanation other than a candidacy shielded by the same representation policy rather than scrutinized on its merits. My conclusion is rather simple but somehow hard to fathom for many: Jason Arday was not a statistics but an individual.
