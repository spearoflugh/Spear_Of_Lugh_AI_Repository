# The Pension under the Skirt

The screen you are looking at right now appears to be a—or perhaps the—window on the world. It is the universal artifact through which people access reality in the digital era. At first glance—pun intended—it seems as though this tool is almighty. There is some truth to that: you do have access to all human knowledge. With AI, it is even better: you have access to an executive summary of any book in the Library of Alexandria. Yet, our contact with reality is becoming increasingly distant. Worse still, initial appearances might consistently function as a veil hiding true reality. By many accounts, there is constantly something akin to a global ‘Tartuffe effect’ at play in our lives.

> *Couvrez ce sein, que je ne saurais voir.*
> *Par de pareils objets les âmes sont blessées,*
> *Et cela fait venir de coupables pensées*
>
> translation: Cover that bosom, which I cannot abide to see. By such sights as these are souls wounded, And they give rise to guilty thoughts.
>
> Tartuffe, ou l'imposteur (1664), III, 2 — Molière

Tartuffe uses the mask of religion, piety, and asceticism to manipulate others and satisfy his material desires (money, power, Orgon's house) and his carnal ones (seducing Elmire). Any resemblance to actual persons, living or dead, or actual events is anything but purely coincidental and it is the subject of this piece.

### Uncovering surprises

Whether you look at France public accounts or Hunter Schafer, reality is not what it appears to be at first glance. It might sound like a joke, but it is not. There is a deep connection between what is hidden behind the books and behind the skirts in our time.

France is experiencing violent unrest that started under the guise of underfunding in education. Yet France (at 5.1% to 5.2% of GDP) stands above neighboring countries such as Germany (around 4.6%–4.8%) or Italy, but below Nordic countries like Sweden (which exceeds 7%) or Iceland. Are these demonstrations a uniquely French phenomenon, then? Well, when you look closer at the books, it turns out that 32% of the French education ministry’s budget does not go toward running schools or teaching students, but is instead spent on pensions for retired civil servants. The French state, like Tartuffe, is constantly displaying the first number while always covering up the second.

Likewise, Hunter Schafer spends her time explaining how she is what she is not: a woman. The underlying structure here is the same as that of the French state’s books. What is displayed is not what is, even though everything is public at the same time. The fact that a third of the budget goes to pensions is public knowledge, in the exact same way that the fact that Hunter Schafer is a biological man is known to everyone. It is precisely this duality that forms the basis of her media presence. One might even be enticed to consider that this exposed lie is her core identity.

I could pile up examples of this phenomenon, where what is displayed is in blatant contradiction with a known reality. Mass migration comes to mind: no one ever campaigned in favor of mass migration, yet it is the policy that has been implemented for decades. But there are even weirder examples. I am picking one simply because of its sheer, face-value absurdity: climate change becoming a major driver behind child marriage in Asia and the Pacific. Yes this is a real article from a “serious” media and is worth reading.

So it appears that blatant lie is not a by product of a digital society but its working engine.

### Thermodynamics

In order to extract work from the universe one has to have sources of different temperatures. That is a fundamental principle of thermodynamics. I used this idea at a metaphysical level and I believe that what was just described in the previous section is another instantiation of this idea.

[Linked post: The Second Law of Metaphysics]

The thesis goes like this: **you cannot extract a narrative from bare facts**. It is a single sentence but it goes deep and needs to be unpacked.

As humans, we make sense of the world through stories. From this perspective, science is not suited to the human psyche. There is no story to be told about numerical predictions. It is simply a matter of observing reality by measuring it. The radius of the hydrogen atom, known as the Bohr radius, is 5.29177210544(82)×10−11 m. That’s it; you can note that it is a small number, but to be frank, there is not much more left to tell.

Transforming a set of facts into a story is what we call history. It is both crucial and elusive. Take the idea of what it means to be French. One way to look at it is from a formalist point of view: it is a bundle of administrative procedures. Another way to view it is through the *“roman national”* (national narrative), which is basically how the French tell themselves what it means to be French. This is somewhat recursive, but not entirely, because new episodes and chapters are being written and rewritten in real time. Facts are incorporated into the story. Thus, the question *“Is the French story true?”* is very hard to answer. One could say that “France as a story” is an illusion, yet it is precisely because this story is believed that people get up early in the morning and go to work—among other things, to pay taxes. It is as if a virtual idea were fleshed out and became more real as time goes by.

So the story of France is a narrative, but not merely a narrative. It is linked to reality and also creates reality. This illustrates the thesis: the story of France is not just a set of bare facts. Nor is it purely an idea, because there are concrete implementations: customs offices, tax services, things you can actually touch with your hands. Moreover, if all French people were to disappear, the idea of France would disappear along with them. One might therefore think that it is an intersubjective phenomenon—a shared idea. But that is not entirely the case, because ideas, whether shared or not, are—from a Platonic perspective—perfect and immune to the passage of time. This is not true for the story of France, which evolves in real time.

The thesis implies an interplay between reality and ideas. Of course, this interplay is mediated through information technologies. Hence a direct question: is what we are observing—this never-ending list of self-evident lies masquerading as the pinnacle of the modern moral high ground—a direct result of the information technology revolution? Or more precisely: how does digital culture impact this all-too-human trait?

### Tartuffe 2.0

The media playing field has been flattened. At least, it appears so because the top-down nature of information broadcasting has become less evident. There is truth to this idea, as even the president is chasing events and struggling to frame what is happening. He appeared very weak in his speech last Sunday (October 18th), and looting continued during the night as if nothing had happened. Yet he still has to project leadership. This is my hypothesis: because he can no longer lead the old way, new ways must be found—much like what Trump did instinctively during the 2016 campaign. Nowadays, this approach has been post-industrialized. By the time people realized that gaining attention required shocking others and provoking outrage, it was already too late. The genie was out of the bottle. And the easiest way to outrage people is to lie right to their faces: you say A with a giant, bright B flashing behind your shoulder; you hold press conferences explaining how much you spend on education while simultaneously publishing data showing that a third of it goes to pensions; you pretend to be the most feminine woman on earth while being a man. Forget about plausible deniability—engagement comes at the expense of integrity. Tartuffe does not want to be caught, but his modern incarnation thrives on it. What can be done about this? I do not know, but literature might once again provide a clue. Here is how Dorine replied to Tartuffe:

> *Vous êtes donc bien tendre à la tentation, Et la chair sur vos sens fait grande impression ? Certes je ne sais pas quelle chaleur vous monte : Mais à convoiter, moi, je ne suis point si prompte, Et je vous verrais nu du haut jusques en bas, Que toute votre peau ne me tenterait pas.*
>
> translation: *You must be very prone to temptation, then, If flesh can stir your senses again and again.I know not what warm passions in you rise, But I am not so quick to feast my eyes; And were you standing naked from top to toe, Your skin would leave me totally cold, I know.*
>
> Tartuffe, ou l’imposteur (1664), III, 2 — Molière
