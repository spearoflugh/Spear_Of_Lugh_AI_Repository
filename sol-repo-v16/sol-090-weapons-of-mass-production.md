# Weapons of Mass Production

The start of this new academic year sees a blossoming of affairs linked to the very heart of this Substack project. From the resolution of a Millennium Prize Problem to the unanimous praise of an AI-produced slop-novel by the French intelligentsia, by way of the meltdown of world-renowned mathematicians wondering about the meaning of life now that AI can prove non trivial theorems. The switch to a digital culture is brutal, and the consequences follow as surely as the leaves will fall from the trees in late autumn. AI is changing fast and introduces the possibility to produce content and it is more destabilizing than I thought.

In one of my first articles on this journey, I reflected on how society was transforming because of digital technologies. Here is a quote from my 2020 essay, “*Ergodicity and Digital Culture*”

> In the digital era writing, publishing, modifying, updating and spreading information has a marginal cost of 0: once you have bought the infrastructure (a computer and access to the network) the fact that you publish do not bring extra costs (not like printing a new copy of a book which at least requires sheets and ink). The vetting process will adapt: it is not before publication but after publication that it will take place.

The events of the past few weeks show that the first sentence of this quote should be revised to be more precise:

In the digital era writing, publishing, modifying, updating, spreading and ***producing*** information has a marginal cost 0.

Indeed, in many fields, AI now adds production to the list of new properties of information in a digital age. It is true that production is not yet marginally zero-cost (the Katechon has been severely wounded, but is not dead yet) and that the cost associated with tokens remains a major issue, but from a functional point of view, we are getting closer. The production costs of a theorem (Maths), an application (software), or a novel (literature) are following the same path as the cost per kilogram to Low Earth Orbit with the SpaceX revolution: they are dropping at an astronomical rate.

When I wrote “*Ergodicity and Digital Culture*” the AIs were not good enough to produce human level texts. They are now acting as weapons of mass production in the world of ideas and it represents another systemic shock that compounds with all the other ones that don’t disappear. I am going to explore the implications of this remark in this piece. The idea of weapons of mass production came to me after reading this post by  on X:

### The end of scarcity

Midas is cursed because everything he touches turns to gold. While it satisfies his greed, it makes it much harder for him to eat. This is more or less what is happening in the world of ideas at the time of writing: it appears that, whatever the field, whatever is touched by AI is instantly transformed. At first, Midas is thrilled because he loves gold. It is only moments later that he realizes that what he found magical—the realization of his deepest desires—is the very thing that will cost him his life. Abundance comes with its own share of problems. Those issues are not those of scarcity (starvation), but they are nevertheless severe (obesity is one of the leading causes of death in food-abundant economies).

There is even a meta-issue: humans were genetically selected to solve problems of scarcity. No one ever wonders, “What am I going to have for dinner today?” For most of human history, this was never a question. You ate what you had. Moreover, scarcity is an unambiguous guide: you know what to do when you are hungry. You start hunting, gathering berries, or tending to crops. What to do when you are confronted with an avalanche of choices is altogether different. The way out of starvation may be hard, but it is clear. The way out of abundance is simple only after you have made it through. Before making the right choice, you are tempted by a myriad of possible solutions.

In technical terms: the price signal is well adapted to managing scarcity, but it fails when managing abundance. Similarly, our institutions were built for a written culture. For instance, in France, a law comes into effect as soon as it is published in the *Journal Officiel* (literally the official journal of the French Republic). The same applies to regulations (*Bulletin Officiel*), the land registry (cadastre), and so on. Essentially, State authority has historically relied on controlling *what* is published.

### What’s next?

If institutions are collapsing, what will happen next? Almost nine billion human beings cannot live like hunter-gatherers. Cities are too vast to be governed like tribal villages. Yet, traditional institutions are doomed. The first thought that comes to mind is that tech giants will take control. I explored this concept in “A Maze Inc.” and often discussed the clash between *deep tech* and the *deep state*. The question is: once the battle is over, what will the landscape look like? One initial possibility is that AI will render Tocqueville’s warning in *Democracy in America* even more relevant:

> *But it would seem that if despotism were to be established amongst the democratic nations of our days, it might assume a different character; it would be more extensive and more mild; it would degrade men without tormenting them. I do not question, that in an age of instruction and equality like our own, sovereigns might more easily succeed in collecting all political power into their own hands, and might interfere more habitually and decidedly within the circle of private interests, than any sovereign of antiquity could ever do. But this same principle of equality which facilitates despotism, tempers its rigor. We have seen how the manners of society become more humane and gentle in proportion as men become more equal and alike. When no member of the community has much power or much wealth, tyranny is, as it were, without opportunities and a field of action. As all fortunes are scanty, the passions of men are naturally circumscribed – their imagination limited, their pleasures simple. This universal moderation moderates the sovereign himself, and checks within certain limits the inordinate extent of his desires.*
>
> *“De la Démocratie en Amérique” -*Alexis de Tocqueville Book Four – Chapters VI

As you read this text, imagine that the tyrant is OpenAI, Anthropic, or a consortium of tech giants. One difference with our situation is that while AI will produce whatever you prompt it to do, you have very little control over the machine. You can include instructions in the prompt that will be underplayed, and the machine’s output can easily be tweaked. This manipulation remains mostly invisible to the user. Consider again the affair surrounding the AI-produced book: even professional editors and critics were fooled. This means that any user can be fooled by an AI on any subject. Of course, if you control the consortium, you will subtly control everyone’s access to knowledge. Thus, it is not direct like a bureaucratic order; it is, rather, indirect.

The problem is that our institutions have mechanisms to self-correct that will not work in this new setting. I am thinking of the First Amendment: freedom of speech is a way to keep political power in check. But considering what I have just written, tech companies will be able to change your thoughts through your interactions with those magical AIs… So the problem won’t come from speech, but from thought. And how do you enforce a “Zeroth Amendment” along the lines of “freedom of thought”? How could that ever be implemented in real life?

The question of Abundance is the most important one of our days. A famous work is Ezra Klein and Derek Thomson’s “Abundance”. Klein and Thomson call for an abundance that must be built; what I am describing is an abundance that we must now survive.

### OODA loop

What I have described in this piece is only the beginning of the first “O” in the societal OODA loop. Times are changing. Institutions will crumble and a new order will emerge—it is the endless sequence of stories in the Bible, with the rise and fall of empires, kings, and cities. The first step toward reconstruction is to know where we stand—to observe—before working on where to go, and how to orient.

Right now, on the one hand, people are making jokes about editors being fooled; on the other, others are mocking mathematicians because they are shaken by recent results (surely a revenge of the mathematically impaired). But these things are merely the froth on the daydream. It is time to think seriously about how life is going to be transformed and what must be done to avoid dystopia. And no, it will not mean giving the keys to the consortium. This Substack is an exploration of the “O”. It is only the beginning.
