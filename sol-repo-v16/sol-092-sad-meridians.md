# Sad Meridians

The impact of technological revolutions is most clearly seen in wars. Take the industrial revolution: when WWI started, the last wars between peer adversaries were almost half a century old. The Franco-Prussian War dated back to 1870, and the American Civil War was fought in the early 1860s. In both conflicts, the industrial revolution had not yet reached its full power. While the impacts of global supply chains (trains) and mass-produced firepower were visible—perhaps we could even include the Russo-Japanese War at the start of the 20th century—the art of war was still more or less the same as the Napoleonic one. Perhaps a single statistic can help measure how things changed: there were around 170,000 casualties in the entirety of the Russo-Japanese War. This death toll was reached in less than a single month at the start of WWI. The total number of casualties in WWI was roughly 30 to 40 times that of the American Civil War.

Institutions are not completely inept, who knew, and the department of War just launched the project Meridian. In a nutshell they are going to spend an untold amount of money to build autonomous weapons etc. They have a problem though: this exuberance in technology is not accompanied with a theory of what war will look like in the future. At best a Sci Fi movie vision.

New information technologies, together with robotics and other advances (for instance, in the space industry), are arguably of an even greater impact than those of the industrial era. Take the example of the smartphone: in less than a decade, every human became connected to the network 24/7. If you consider a larger window, say between 1990 and today, we moved from a handful of academics to near-universal connectivity. Same phenomenon can be witnessed with the adoption rate of AI by both particulars and the corporate world.

The world has not been through wars between top-of-the-food-chain peers for decades now. Perhaps the Korean War is the closest thing to such an encounter. The Falklands conflict was narrow, and the Iran-Iraq War was not between two top-notch countries in terms of military might. Likewise, neither Gulf War qualifies, because the power disparity between the USA and Iraq was far too large.

​This is mostly due to the freezing effect of the Cold War: two nuclear-armed superpowers made this kind of direct confrontation far too risky. Nowadays we seem to be in a more multi-polar world with many centers of power. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 marked the beginning of this new era. The question is, what has been learned from this war? And more importantly, what do those lessons—coupled with the recent evolution of the tech sector—herald for the future of warfare? I will explore these questions by examining four sub-issues one after the other in no particular order. The points that I have identified are the following (I first explain the idea in a single sentence then ponder longer on the subject later in the piece):

1. **Supply chains and economic imbalance:** An interceptor costs an order of magnitude more than the price of what it intercepts.
2. **Technological singularity:** The pace of technological change now outlasts the shelf life of stockpiled weapons, making agility more valuable than mass.
3. **Dual-purpose technology:** Most technologically advanced tools can serve both civil and military purposes.
4. **Topological revolutions:**A growing dissociation between geographical places and effects.

### **1-Supply chains and economic imbalance**

Facts are blunt, the price of a typical air-to-air missile is in the ballpark of the million of dollars while the price of a low grade drone, shahed/geran style is rather around few thousand of dollars. Clearly it is not sustainable on the long run to shoot down drones with overpriced missiles that were supposed to be used against much more valuable targets like bombers or other fighter jets.

Yet things are not that simple. First, the low cost drone can cause damages of the order of magnitude of the price of the interceptor. Typically shutting down an international airport for an hour can cost around few millions of dollars. Second, things are never stationary during a conflict: interceptors are just a part of a large system whose aim is to suppress the possibility of the adversary to launch drones forever. So interceptors make sense in a conflict that is not one of attrition.

If the conflict somehow freezes, as it does in the Ukraine Russia war, the number of targets will diminish quickly and a kind of large dead zone appears in which drones from both parties are loitering and the use of interceptor becomes marginal.

**Practical suggestion:**once installed it is very hard to dislodge a drone hedgehog. The implication is clear: one has to rush to make the installation of a hedgehog the most difficult possible. One way to do it is to project troops without a lot of support, but equipped with those drones with the sole strategy to sow chaos. It is linked to point 3 because dual purpose tech is going to be used by troops that can’t be logistically supported as it was the case for 20th-century troops. It means that infiltrated troops can’t really do precise things, thus their role is simpler and requires less coordination. Compare with an operation like WWII Market-Garden operation in which ground troops were supposed to join with airborne troops. A modern version would let the airborne troops sow chaos on their own: so less troops and acting in a more covert way with a very simple rule of engagement, make the place impossible for organized efforts.

### 2-Technological Singularity

Another key aspects of logistics is preparedness. 20th-century military apparatus were stockpiling huge amount of food, ammunition, be ready to increase the number of soldiers manifold. In early 1914, just prior to the outbreak of the war, the active peacetime French Army numbered roughly 800,000 to 825,000 men. Within the first couple of weeks, France mobilized an astonishing 3.7 to 4.2 million men. It is not by magic. Things were ready to absorb such an influx of soldiers. French army possessed an ammunition stockpile of approximately 5 million rounds for its primary weapon—the famous *Canon de 75 mm modèle 1897* field gun.
While 5 million rounds sounds massive, it reflects one of the greatest military miscalculations of the early 20th century:

- Massive Underestimation: French pre-war military doctrine and logistical planning expected a short, mobile war of movement. Their estimates assumed an army consumption rate of roughly 100,000 to 130,000 shells a month. Under those calculations, a 5-million-round reserve felt like an overwhelmingly safe cushion to last a long campaign.
- The Reality of Trench Warfare: Once the Battle of the Marne settled into static trench warfare, the consumption rate exploded. A single day of heavy artillery bombardment by both sides could consume tens of thousands—and eventually hundreds of thousands—of shells.

A first idea: the doctrine was made before the war started and officers had in mind the previous wars. They did not anticipated well the new environment for warfare. By late autumn 1914, French batteries were facing critical shortages, forcing the government to desperately ration ammunition, recall obsolete black-powder artillery from fortresses, and rapidly militarize civilian factories to prevent a total military collapse. Over the course of the entire war, French industry would go on to manufacture over 200 million 75 mm shells alone.
A second idea has to be taken into account: the shells at the start of the war were the same ones at the end of the war. Compare with what is happening in the Ukraine-Russia war (which lasted as long as WW1 now). Drone models change every few weeks, tactics and technological capabilities too. One example: the length of fiber optic cables went from 5/10 km in 2024 to advanced long-range platforms (such as Ukraine’s Ptashka 17-O) feature spools stretching up to 50 kilometers to strike targets deep behind enemy lines.
A third idea is linked to the first point: there is a good chance that interceptors become obsolete way before having been used a lot. A recent example is the Geran-4 drone, which is an evolution of the Shahed that now uses a jet engine. This innovation makes obsolete many interceptor technologies developed by Ukraine that worked for earlier propeller-driven models. It makes the economic imbalance between offense and defense even more pronounced.

**Practical suggestion:** The future militaro-industrial complex should rather look like silicon-valley/tech model than a purely industrial one. What does it concretely mean? It means that the ecosystem is constantly moving and changing: compare with the long term investment for heavy industries. A practical issue though is to stimulate innovation in the typically military stuff outside of conflicts. Maybe one way to do is to build a bounty-like incentive architecture with continual challenges (and you can make interceptors trying to challenge new drones and vice-versa).

### 3-Dual-Purpose Technology

The advancement of technology has significantly amplified individual power. This trend, already apparent before the digital revolution—as demonstrated by the 9/11 attacks—is even more pronounced today. Consider a First World War infantryman: armed with a rifle and a few grenades, his capabilities were severely limited. Today, anyone anywhere can operate an automaton on the opposite side of the globe simply by using the internet to select from a vast array of drones and other robotic systems to exert influence. Furthermore, network access provides ordinary citizens with real-time information that generals could only have dreamed of two or three decades ago.

If you zoom out, the new information technologies enable group of several people to synchronize their efforts on a scale that was only achievable through professional armies with a top down hierarchy. It is therefore not clear that a professional army could defeat a particularly united adversary. Just consider the recent example of the Houthis who have withstood a full fledged attack by the US who gave up at least (because Houthis have entered the conflict with Iran and God only knows what will happen in the short term) the first round in March to May 25 bombing campaign.  Likewise the ongoing war between Iran and the USA/Israel offers a clear example of staunch resistance by the presumably weaker part. The famous mosaic defense strategy relies on this dual nature of technology. Instead of encrypted military tactical radios, the mosaic defense force leverage everyday technology that is hard for an invader to shut down without crippling their own occupation or alienating the local population. It can also been seen in the Ukraine war with the multiple episodes around Starlink.

**Practical suggestion:**The operational defense of territory shall play on the dual purpose of new technologies. What is important is to prepare the plans and the unity of defenders. A mosaic approach with a defense in depth appears very hard to neutralize even by a more well equipped and organized adversary. The Ukrainian example may come to mind with the resilience of the power grid (that was targeted by russian forces for months) on top of what happened with the communication infrastructures. There is also a link with point 1: ensuring the continuation of the logistics is more important than an airtight defense ensuring that no adversary can penetrate the territory.

### 4-Topological Revolutions

This point is far more abstract than the others, and arguably even more crucial, as it encompasses both physical and conceptual realms. The core premise is that traditional notions of distance and borders have been radically transformed by new information technologies. To summarize: throughout human history, words and swords traveled at the same pace. This no longer holds true, as a social media post can be read instantaneously anywhere in the world, effectively rendering the distance for ideas negligible. On a secondary level, human mobility has undergone a similar shift; affordable air travel enables migrants to move freely between countries. Contrast this with traveling to the Americas by ship before World War II—a definitive one-way journey where daily communication with family back home was impossible.

​Militarily, this implies that once-insurmountable logistical hurdles are now easily and cheaply overcome. Furthermore, while projecting power was historically restricted to the limits of direct line-of-sight, the advent of orbital strike systems—as highlighted by the Department of Defense/War—means that theoretically any point on Earth is vulnerable to a kinetic attack. In this sense, orbital strike systems are the military counterpart to social media. The transformation of regional proximity is further demonstrated by the ongoing conflict involving the US, Israel, and Iran. Despite sharing no borders and deploying no ground troops, a high-intensity war persists between them.

​This dynamic introduces a shift in our perception of time: warfare is now intermittent. Unlike 20th-century total wars characterized by continuous combat until total capitulation, modern conflicts feature short bursts of violence interspersed with prolonged periods of complete kinetic absence. Consequently, the state of being ‘at war’ has lost its traditional clarity. In the virtual and informational domain, numerous hybrid and disinformation operations further illustrate that conventional borders have dissolved.

**Practical suggestion**: The notion of territorial control has to change. This is not directly a practical suggestion, but it is deeply important. How militaries are going to manage the absence of land control is going to change radically. This issue is at least acknowledged in the Hegseth Quantico speech on September the 30th: projects “Fortress America” and “Next Great American Base” are starting points directly linked to the topological revolution.  Remember that operations  like Spiderweb or the infiltration of Russian troops in the Kharkiv Oblast last June are just early examples. Similarly, one cannot ignore the two surprise bombing campaigns launched by the US against Iran while negotiations were underway. Pearl Harbor-style operations are becoming the norm rather than the exception.
