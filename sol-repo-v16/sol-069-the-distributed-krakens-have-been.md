# The Distributed Krakens have been released

I want to examine further the distributed system aspects that our lives are taking under the digital revolution. I have already written about that subject here and there. The interested reader might find this context interesting, but I will make this piece as standalone as possible.

​What I call a distributed system is something best explained by the following context on social media. Notice that it is not even a real conversation. One has to picture hundreds of thousand  interleaving posts following this scheme:

1. ​It is ok to punch a Nazi.
2. ​If you say X, you are a Nazi.
3. ​Y said something that is X-adjacent.
4. ​Y deserved what happened to him because 1.

​You have spotted the context pre-C. Kirk assassination in 1, 2, and 3. The 4 was the justification post-assassination that was witnessed over social media. Now, each one of 1, 2, 3, and 4 can say, with their hand over their heart, “we never called for political violence.” 1 is simply exploring philosophical questions like “is it moral to kill Hitler in 1908 if you had the chance to?”. 2 is assessing the quality of speech, also an abstract activity, right? 3 is just stating an opinion on a public statement. 4 is deploring the violence in the first sentence just in order to justify it by producing fifteen times the disclaimer amount of text explaining why it was a good thing after all (usually the disclaimer consists of the first and last sentence, in a very LLM-esque approach). So no one is to blame and everyone can now dance over the grave of C. Kirk. Political activists lived happily ever after and had many children. Wait. No, they aren’t going to have many children; they are rather going to be part of many political events.

Of course, this is an archetypal presentation, but it enlightens a lot of recent trends. A strength of distributed systems is that they produce massive results by synchronizing a multitude of systems. By this, they produce results that couldn’t have been obtained otherwise (say, using a very large data center). The Distributed Denial of Service attack is a rogue way to flood a server. To do that, hackers first gather an army of robots that will be used to send those blocking requests. By their sheer number, and by the fact that the requests come from everywhere (so blocking a single point isn’t going to help), those stupid robots will submerge the attacked service.

​What I am going to talk about in this piece is how a similar distributed attack is used as a new way to control the narrative. The image of a totalitarian state controlling speech is, for the layman, that of censorship and surveillance. Something like the 1984 version: Big Brother, etc. What is actually happening is different and disconcerting. It is also a bit frightening because it shows that, by many measures, we have slipped into a strange kind of authoritarian way of life without ever having noticed it. Mostly because those techniques are new, and we lack the words and concepts to tackle them.

### Release the baits

The global conversation is best described by what is not said. At least since the JFK assassination, the development of conspiracy theories has been fueled by half-revelations and chosen silences by institutions. From UFOs, for which we had a very recent release of data, to 9/11, passing by the mental state of the POTUS and various egregious claims around the origin of the COVID-19 virus and the Nord Stream sabotage, you can gather dozens of major stories for which the normal ways of public discussion appear weird at first glance. The point is not to get lost in a specific story or to discuss specific details, but to spot the structure. Because that is the trap.

​The trap is a distributed system to protect a narrative. Just like in the C. Kirk scheme. It works like this:

1. ​Organize a press conference in which you repeat what has been seen by everybody, but in a solemn form. Just stick to material aspects and never touch on motivation.
2. ​If motivation could be part of a discussion—say, a manifesto has been published—then hide it under the guise of “an investigation is ongoing.”
3. ​Pepper the first press conferences with outrageously stupid snippets like: “That building in particular has a sloped roof at its highest point,” and refuse to elaborate.
4. ​Wait for weirdos to do their thing online.
5. ​Rinse and repeat until the news cycle is over.
6. ​Dozens of months or years later, promise to release the files. Release a fraction of the data, heavily redacted.
7. ​Wait and enjoy.

​The famous recent releases around the Epstein files, JFK files, or UAP files all followed the scheme. Not a single definitive conclusion was possible to draw in each case. Obvious questions coming from the documents are just ignored and left for the public to ponder: “How did no one investigate this or that?”. This is a very good recipe to enhance confirmation bias. Moreover, by disseminating a huge amount of data that simultaneously never contains conclusive evidence, the institutions ensure that conspiracies are going to blossom.

​Institutions make no effort to build a coherent narrative; they will just point at inconsistencies here and there and call it nonsensical. Notice how the scheme has been reversed. Before, it was the citizens who were calling their government liars, incapable, etc. Nowadays, it is the government that, while sitting on tons of data, is mocking the citizen for having weird theories. Yet they won’t explain anything, or when they do, they will do it in such a way that no one could take their words at face value. Once again, think of the Secret Service head talking about the “particular sloped roof” supposedly forbidding them from inspecting the area...

​What is interesting here is that institutions are just setting the stage while the public is doing the job. Talk about an efficient architecture of incentives. This is the distributed system aspect of the powers that be. From a top-down control of the narrative—say, implementing agents in the editorial boards of the NYT or WaPo—they have pivoted to the destruction of every narrative that could challenge them by promoting suspicion.

### Distributed authoritarianism

The fact that authoritarianism finds new ways to express itself using digital technologies is not surprising. After all, the idea that technology is agnostic has some merits. It may appear counterintuitive at first sight because of the inherent multiplicity of actors. Historically, authoritarianism rather rhymed with “one” than with “many.” Different actors having opposed viewpoints were supposed to equilibrate, and Mandeville’s “Fable of the Bees: or, Private Vices, Public Benefits” was precisely about how individualistic behavior was at the root of the wealth of the whole community. What we have seen is that, as often, digital technologies invert that scheme. The powers that be can trigger individualities to hamper the synchronization of the public. By making the construction of a shared narrative impossible, the opposition is killed in the bud.

The Krakens have now been released all over the world. They have eaten every semblance of sanity in the global discourse.

While this technique is very efficient, it comes with its own poison: by doing so, institutions aren’t able anymore to impose their own agenda. It is something like an autoimmune disorder. The calls like “democracy is on the ballot” are losing strength. This is another fable, one of Aesop, that is of use here: the boy who cried wolf. By calling out conspiracy at every turn, the institutions have lost the veneer of respectability they had before switching to digital culture. Toxicity.
