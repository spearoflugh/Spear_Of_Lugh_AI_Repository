# The Draft for the Drift

Over what important topic have you changed your mind recently? Let's be more precise, with ‘recently’ meaning ‘since the advent of globalized social media’. Say a decade, more or less, at least post-2016. Anything that comes to your mind? Are you able to remember a day, a specific event where it happened? Was it a slow process, a gradual evolution? Or was it rather that one day you woke up, and like Gregor Samsa, you had turned into a monstrous vermin to your previous self?

[Copyright note: the opening sentence of an English translation of Kafka's "The Metamorphosis" quoted by the author in the original post — omitted from this AI repository. See the source URL for the full text.]

Lately, I have experienced both the gradual drift and the abrupt versions. Of course, the nonsense during COVID-19 was rather brutal and hard to ignore. There were overnight stupidities enforced into laws or regulations, together with temporary exceptional powers granted by an emergency status that dragged on over weeks and months.

I have the intuition that in our social media culture, we are rather living through the incremental and, more importantly, subconscious way of evolving about important topics. There are technological reasons behind this, not only bad actors.

### Revolving stories

People gather their news primarily from social media and no longer from newspapers. There is a huge difference: while the newspaper of the day is timestamped and the same for every reader, timelines are evanescent and different for every subscriber. Plus, nobody—except for reasons related to cancel culture—ever looks at tweets older than a week. The half-life of a tweet is in minutes, not hours or days. So, the mechanical implication is that you no longer have a fixed reference to the past, at least with regard to the global conversation. The timeline is like a caterpillar for ideas: it flattens everything and eliminates every asperity.

This is the perfect recipe for drifting. Because, on one hand, you have news cycles that are constantly shifting, and on the other, no clear references to the past. The war in Iran is going to become hot again, and winter is coming, followed by a new summer with cold and heat waves. In between, the return of the S. Zakas of the world will see the conversation switch to totally different subjects. Discontinuity is the name of the game. Do you remember those thriller movies from the 90s in which the heroes go to the municipal library to look at old newspapers on microfilm to find the trail of a serial killer? Those days are over. The heroes won’t go further than a Google search tailored to their profiles. They will change their minds and turn their attention toward a notification or a TikTok dance before even reaching page two of the search engine where the results sits, if it has been included in the results of the search at all. Moreover, there is already this list of explanations ready to use. From information to confirmation through digital communication.

### Omnicauses

There is a heatwave in France right now. This has become the "omnicause" for the week. If your bus is late or the subway is not working, it is because of the heat. Just like during the darkest days of the COVID-19 crisis, you have the daily display of broken records. You can picture the situation because it is a repetitive scheme. One peculiarity of France is the media presence of Serge Zaka. He found a way to get noticed with his "Crocodile Dundee" style while being a researcher on climate. He is the omniexplainer, jumping from one TV broadcast to another. The same type of omniexplainer exists for international relations; you can bet he was everywhere during the hot instants of the Iran conflict. The job of these omniexplainers is to serve you the omniexplanation in a digitally palatable form:

1. **Graphically identifiable in 0.1 second**. It is like the Yellow Vest protests, the subject must be understandable from a single picture. Essentially, an emotional widening of the thirst trap technique.
2. **Production of 15 to 20-seconds soundbites**. This is the perfect length to generate viral social media “controversies”. Attention is good, but reaction is better because it promises future attention.
3. **Use of simple language**. There is no time to get into layered argumentation or explanations of technical or precise terms. Just like a strike with smart ammunition, the payload has to be delivered with an economy of means.
4. **Moralizing in absolute terms**. the competition for attention being what it is, if you don’t use raw instincts like fear, disgust, or sexual arousal, you are going to lose the attention-seeking race in the social media jungle. Therefore, you have to fight “evil”—nothing less—in each of your interventions.

There is a direct relation to this drift: if the events are constantly changing, the analytical framework does not change and remains relatively independent of reality. It is because if you are an omniexplainer you are never wrong. Right?  It might appear paradoxical: if the analytical framework is frozen, how does it produce drift? Mainly because you have to pay lip service to remain heard. A very good example is the evolution of the discussion on vaccination during the COVID-19 crisis. At the time, one of the most popular omniexplainers was A. Fauci. The moral stage was set such that vaccination became synonymous with “the good” (in the religious understanding of the term).

Then came the discussion on the efficacy, safety, and mandatory aspects of different vaccines. I am not going to replay the match, but you can go back to recorded broadcasts. At each point in time certainty was displayed, yet you can check how the consensus has evolved around those questions. It started with the “church of the 9s”—there was this race by laboratories to add nines to the efficacy percentages of their products. Then it became clear that vaccines were not protecting against infection. It became: “but you will not propagate the disease,” which also rapidly was shown to be inaccurate. ### The Red Queen’s race

In *Through the Looking-Glass*, there is a scene which involves both the Red Queen, a representation of a Queen in chess and Alice constantly running but remaining in the same spot, it is called the Red Queen Race:

> “Well, in *our* country,” said Alice, still panting a little, “you’d generally get to somewhere else—if you ran very fast for a long time, as we’ve been doing.”
>
> “A slow sort of country!” said the Queen. “Now, *here*, you see, it takes all the running *you* can do, to keep in the same place. If you want to get somewhere else, you must run at least twice as fast as that!”
>
> *Through the looking* - Lewis Carroll

This is a very good allegory for the drift mechanism on social media. To keep the interpretative framework of the world unchanged, one needs to produce a lot of content and forgot a lot. It is like being on a treadmill and having to adapt to changing speed just not to fall down or being ejected. It is a kind of perpetual motion that produces no change. Has Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus noticed that his position was always presented with certainty while it evolved in front of our eyes? Was he spending so much energy just to stay relevant that he wasn’t able to amend himself? No one can answer this but himself. I will leave it to the reader to examine how he responded to the draft — and to ask the same question of themselves.
