# Broken Filters

I am still recovering from the intellectual fallout of the massive OpenAI dump of 700 preprints on October 6th. Many ideas are swirling at once, and it is far too early to tell what’s what. The landscape looks like ground zero after a thermonuclear detonation. I could cite dozens of examples, but I will limit myself to two—my goal here is not to document those reactions (a fascinating and necessary effort, though), but rather to explore what this means for the future. The first is a talk (in French) by Fields Medalist Hugo Duminil-Copin, who explains that overnight, all of his research projects were headshotted (for french speakers, the talk is available here).

The second one appears lighter, formatted as a meme, but is very telling. Among the published results, there is one on the optimal algorithmic complexity of multiplication. The initial result was ugly—something like n(log n)^{1-2^{-182}}, so almost n log n, but slightly better. 'Better' here means that it scales like 0.9999\dots (with 48 nines) times n log n. The improvement over n log n, which was believed to be the optimal case, is just ridiculous (you might save a couple of CPU cycles on a single huge multiplication by using the entire world's computing power). In the following hours, mathematicians kept improving the result faster and faster, which is the story behind this meme:

[Embedded X post]

This is all fun and good but those are surface level events. October 6 appears as a serious contender for the day when the written culture toppled. Future will tell if historians elect that day.

### The toppling of the editorial function

Toppling statues is never a neutral act. What just happened in mathematics is the equivalent. In a single batch, OpenAI released more results than a Fields Medalist could dream of producing in a lifetime. Even better, some specific results are worthy of a entire lifetime’s work.

We have been living in a written culture more or less since Jesus was born: the very existence of a concept like ‘People of the Book’ is telling in itself. The defining feature of written culture, as opposed to oral culture, is the preeminence of the editorial function (in oral culture, it is deference to the elders who hold knowledge and pass it down to younger generations). Essentially, what was paramount was the power to decide what got written. Indeed, writing things down was difficult, and revising them once published was harder still. Remember, the Ten Commandments were literally carved in stone.

The importance of the editorial function is most pronounced in mathematics because, at the end of the day, mathematicians only produce texts. And the sole interest of these texts lies in whether they are correct or not. Unlike physics, biology, or engineering, not a single atom of the universe is involved in the process. It is entirely about ideas and their validity. Therefore, it is only logical that mathematics is the first domain to be toppled.

### Falling trees in the forest

An intriguing point—at least for me—is how uninteresting this appears to the average Joe. I have discussed it, and it barely makes non-mathematicians bat an eye. Most haven’t even heard about it. I know I am in a unique position regarding this subject, as I am a mathematician who has worked on topics directly impacted by the release, and my feed on X reflects that (I follow and interact with mathematicians a lot). Yet I remember that when Andrew Wiles proved Fermat’s Last Theorem or when Garry Kasparov lost to Deep Blue, the media coverage was completely different. What happened on the 6th is orders of magnitude bigger than both of those events combined. A whole branch of academia has seen its *raison d’être* severely smashed overnight. It is not even an exaggeration to state that a teenager equipped with enough tokens can now produce more than the best mathematician working without AI. It is not clear whether professionals will benefit enough from AI to truly set themselves apart from that kid.

It is even worse than when Kasparov lost to Deep Blue. As time has shown, humans prefer playing chess against other humans. This means there is still room for a professional scene, because people are more interested in watching Magnus Carlsen play (and make the occasional blunder) than seeing flawless, heartless games played between two computers. But in mathematics? There is no opponent, nor is there a human element. Either a result is correct or it isn’t. Mathematicians don’t even have drama on their side.

This might seem unrelated to everyday life, but that is a superficial take on what this situation illustrates. Grants awarded to certain research programs have effectively been rendered obsolete overnight. If you think this won’t affect the next round of grant allocations, think again. The number of politicians who will seize this opportunity to freeze research funding is certainly not zero. The entire academic world has been turned upside down.

Mathematicians are many things but stupid. Terrence Tao immediately spotted the issue, and  the whole class is in state of shock. I can understand the following declaration, even if very unfortunate:

The quote “Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done” will remain as a turning point. It is funny and sad at the same time. It sounds like a “not like that” meme. The call for OpenAI boycott is also understandable even if childish. I am sorry Terrence but if you don’t use AI you are going to look like those Sentinelese people who never made contact with the rest of humanity.

### The first will be the first

Do you remember the female reactions to the Sydney Sweeney commercials? Expect the same kind of reaction from midwits toward top-tier mathematicians. Now that anyone with enough motivation can produce world-class research, the revenge will be relentless. Just like Instagram influencers using filters on their photos, mediocre mathematicians will use AI to outshine smarter colleagues. Imagine the repercussions. On top of that, mathematics has always been an elitist field—to a degree that only an unattractive woman sitting next to Sydney Sweeney could truly fathom

But they won’t be laughing for long. AI is coming for them, too. Again, mathematicians are many things, but stupid isn’t one of them. They will adapt, because I believe there is still room for ingenuity and creativity. As the first to be hit, they will be the first to adapt—and they will do it faster and better. Midwits will remain midwits. Sydney without makeup or filters is still Sydney, and deep down, everyone knows it. Life is unfair, and even AI can’t make it fair.
