# Pandora's Parabox

Unboxing is a relatively recent phenomenon. The first recorded unboxing video dates back a little less than two decades—and it was about a Nokia phone, mind you! Every second of the 1:26-minute video is worth watching, right down to the 64-megabyte memory stick. Unboxing later became a full-fledged cultural genre in its own right.

Sharing content based entirely on feeling is a highly specific digital activity. While the informational payload of such content is low, the real point is to transmit emotion and a sense of “truthiness.” Take a look at the video that started it all:

[Embedded video]

As we spend more and more time in front of our screens, we have lost eye contact and genuine emotional connection. The digital revolution hasn’t yet succeeded in quenching this thirst. Let’s take a look at how this craving manifests within the digital revolution.

### Gonzo

In the adult film industry, “Gonzo” is a strictly defined genre that completely transformed production methods starting in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Before the advent of gonzo, traditional adult films tried to mimic Hollywood, relying on scripts, budgets, lighting rigs, music, and highly artificial plots—such as the cliché pizza delivery or plumber storylines. Gonzo stripped all of that away. Instead, a plotless structure, a handheld active camera, the breaking of the fourth wall, and the illusion of raw reality became the fundamental elements combined to define the genre.

If this structure appears equivalent to that of an unboxing video, it is because the digital era has fostered a fierce competition for attention. There is simply no time to waste on superfluous context. Going straight to the heart of the action is the name of the game, regardless of the medium. Gonzo and unboxing are both perfect examples of ticks without semantics that I examined in  “The Synchronized Symphony”

Shock value or surprise is used to hook the viewer—think of a “grandma listening to heavy metal” type of hook—and the remaining content is pure emotion, almost entirely devoid of actual information. It goes from racial rage-baiting, to cute pet passing by outrageous food clips.

The physical package is also the perfect metaphor for a kind of content that has strictly defined boundaries. It fits the exact canvas of your preferred social media platform, perfectly illustrating the digital Pandora’s parabox: while human emotions are inherently intimate and unique, they are increasingly delivered to us in uniform, standardized dosages.

### Fatigue

This constant avalanche of content, along with its accompanying emotional roller coaster, has taken over social media entirely. It is now incredibly difficult to maintain on social media a sustained conversation on any complex subject. Of course, Twitter was always Twitter—meaning it always had its fair share of trolls and stubbornly evidence-proof users—but I remember a time when that wasn't the absolute norm. Just as the adult film industry continuously optimized its product, social media platforms have also converged toward a unified format designed to maximize revenue.

Yet, humans are more than just emotional platforms. Some of us think—or at least attempt to. For the thinking mind, the social media circus resembles the opening of Pandora’s box. Driven by sheer curiosity, every primal emotion has been unleashed upon the world. In the original myth, the only thing remaining at the bottom of the box is hope. While digital technologies arrived with the promise of enhanced human connection—giving us unprecedented tools to exchange ideas globally and instantaneously—the reality is quite different. Somehow, it has culminated in a landscape where the vast majority of broadcasted content is entirely sub-verbal. TikTok dances and mindless challenges effortlessly eclipse a well-crafted piece of writing. Just as in the myth, it remains unclear whether hope is a blessing or a curse. Does it represent our salvation from misery, or is it simply another cruel trick designed to deepen human despair?

### Reboxing

As an optimist, I will stick to the former. The question is: what does the future look like from this perspective? We are now in the midst of a mutation from **a** written culture to a digital culture. It looks like we are stuck in the worst of both worlds: the editorial function has declined and lost all legitimacy, while the social media way of communicating seems locked in a thirst trap. I have advocated for years now that the solution is going to come from a new approach to reputation but also to communication. For instance in this Substack I have built an AI repository of my most important articles published. The idea is to use it as a context to access the content of this blog over the years without having to read 250 articles. I don’t know whether or not it is going to be massively adopted but it is the opposite of the unboxing thing: the hope is to put things into a box so that they can be manipulated at will and not by being a victim of biological impulses.
