# The Rummaging Area

At the same time that having interactions on social media is becoming more difficult, the back-and-forth with AI is becoming easier. The just-introduced daily limits on the number of posts, replies, and direct messages on X are a glaring example of this global trend. Of course, the surface explanation is that it is a move to counter bots. I discussed this very idea in these columns three years ago, but the fact that it applies to unverified accounts makes it dubious. Indeed, bots and their owners just have to pay a small fee to be free from those limits. More and more 'verified accounts' have now morphed into click farms with the massive help of AI-redacted content. Something like:

It is not the internet we dreamed of. It is the internet we have.

The mechanical result of this general move is the disappearance of room for thinking and toying around with ideas in the general discussion. It is an attack on free speech of a new nature and it is the subject of this piece.

### Freedom to tinker

The technology blog from Princeton was named *Freedom to Tinker*; it is now called the CITP blog. It is a shame, because the name was excellent. By the way, just as X is better known as the former Twitter, the CITP blog is subtitled 'formerly Freedom to Tinker.' This loss of tinkering is perhaps one of the most salient evolutions of the digital world. In the 90s, internet culture was by nature fringe and rebellious. It was a bit naive, too, with things like the *Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace* by J.P. Barlow. It has now been taken over by 'normies'—a kind of regression toward the mean. I can’t help but think that part of it stems from the increasing use of AI. These tools are now the primary means of producing social media click farms. And what are AIs, if not statistical tools by nature, offering a glorified average as the pinnacle of thought?

Tinkering is the exact opposite. It is an activity where things are half done, like a tweet half well written, and where the exploration aspect takes precedence over the form. The AI products are the exact opposite: an average text wrapped up in a shiny formulation. Plus the act of producing is not very challenging, a prompt is by definition small and does not bear a lot of information. It is obtained flawlessly at the first try. Not a lot of tinkering is involved.

It wouldn’t be that bad if it didn’t have such a global effect. Genuine posts are buried under an avalanche of robot-produced content. It is as if AI were a kind of foam occupying every empty space in the global conversation. Like foam, AI content is mostly empty, yet it occupies a great deal of space. If a user wants to engage in interactions, there is little choice now: either you participate in threads generated by AI from accounts that aggregate high activity, or you are screaming into the void. I remember a Twitter that wasn't like that, a place where 'rummaging areas' existed. You could start mid-sized conversations on almost any subject. Today’s social media platforms are different. Of course, the algorithm and its evolution are a factor, but I think users have co-evolved, too. It might sound strange, but I see in this evolution a warning for our societies in which the fertility rate is dropping.

### Fertility and rurarility

The fertility rate is crashing all around the globe. Most countries are now below the replacement level. Some extreme examples, like South Korea, might already be past the tipping point from which they cannot recover. What does this mean in practical terms? Large cities like New York are still full of opportunities, but on the other hand, you can literally buy small towns in remote parts of Kentucky. Same thing can be done in vast areas in Europe in Italy and Spain, France also.

This is where the comparison with large accounts on social media comes in. The decline in fertility rates is producing big winners and a myriad of small 'losers.' Just like the small accounts you never hear about, there are ghost towns appearing everywhere now. To an urbanized person on the net, both are invisible. The phenomenon can be measured in other ways: when I started to play chess … I had to go to a local chess club to play. Of course the online game has killed the associative life. The discrepancy in utility is just too enormous. It is not just a bit better or more convenient. By the way, a true anecdote, when I was explaining to my parents what internet was in 1992 I told them something like “it allows me to play chess with someone in Japan”.

It can be seen as a discontinuity in the scaling: there is nothing left between the very large and the individual. Something like a severed Jacob’s Ladder. The landscape is rather like a teleportation between those two levels without intermediary states. The angels are no longer moving back and forth on the ladder. They appear and disappear on the earth and in heaven. Just like the apparition of the LLM content after a small prompt. The work of thinking and crafting sentence is avoided.

Both the fertility rate evolution and the change in social media mood are not well understood. They can be observed but only with the right angle. If you live in a mega city and don’t look at fertility numbers you can absolutely live a normal life without ever being aware of the ghostification of the countryside. Likewise, if you never used the internet before the 2000s, because of your age, you have no idea of how the landscape has morphed.

Occasionally you have clashes of the ancient and digital worlds. I remember this thing around Osama bin laden letter being ‘rediscovered’ by TikTok crowd in 2023. Likewise does Barlow’s declaration of independence only make sense for zoomers or alphas? I couldn’t tell and they couldn't either. Me because I lived this period and them because they are social media natives. So something has been lost without anyone noticing.

### The thin red line

In the movie The Thin Red Line, the faces of the soldiers become indistinguishable from one another. The voices of their thoughts are also difficult to attribute to any particular individual. It is a form of anonymity that recalls the digital anonymity of social media as we just described it. This anonymity is not chosen, but rather imposed on individuals by circumstances; it departs from the liberating anonymity of the 90s internet. That earlier anonymity was freeing and was one of the ingredients of the tinkering that took place at the time. Somewhere between that era and our present, we might find a 'thin red line' where things have flipped upside down.

Is there a way to escape from the digital maze? The most common response is to desperately search for originality, and this impulse is at the root of the deluge of mindless short videos and other competitive trends. The ‘rummaging area’ must be recreated by individuals for themselves, not for an audience, and not simply in search of reactions. Tinkering is a conversation with the universe, and an awareness of its feedback
